## Reading the book for this power board

Bogatin's strongest transferable idea is that connectivity alone is a thin definition of a correct PCB. The schematic does not encode the physical return path, current density, local thermal environment, or the inductance between an IC and its bypass capacitor. A layout should be judged against the *specific noise, heat, and voltage-drop mechanism* it is meant to control. This supports a review organized by current loops and evidence, rather than a generic visual inspection. The author explicitly asks the designer to identify a noise root cause, form an equivalent circuit, estimate its magnitude, and then measure a prototype to check the estimate (printed pp. 79–82 / PDF 80–83; ch. 4, pp. 87–111 / PDF 88–112). For Temper, loop annotation should distinguish hot bridge commutation, gate drive, control supply, quiet measurement, and isolated SELV paths. That classification is a Temper inference; the book does not analyze this exact induction cooker.

The book's examples are mostly 2- and 4-layer low-cost microcontroller prototypes. Its useful generality is the geometry: shorter current-and-return loops reduce inductance; continuous nearby returns reduce coupling; larger copper cross sections reduce resistance and heating; the weakest constriction determines path performance. Its numerical defaults are **not universal limits**. The 6 mil/1 A, 20 mil/3 A and 13 mil via/~3 A examples assume particular 1 oz copper, DC current, board cooling and allowed temperature rise (pp. 126–133 / PDF 127–134). They cannot be used as power-stage ampacity rules for a high-current resonant bridge or as safety clearance rules. Likewise, the preference for two layers, default FR-4, ordinary HASL, or a 4-layer ground-ground stack depends on cost and signal/return priorities (pp. 473–481 / PDF 474–482). The Temper board must justify its own stackup and fabrication process.

An important scope correction is grounding. Bogatin argues against casually splitting a **signal return** plane because a cut can force fast return current into a large loop (pp. 287–292, 465–467 / PDF 288–293, 466–468). That argument does not erase the electrical separation of mains/hot circuitry from SELV, protective earthing requirements, or isolation-barrier creepage. A hot return, SELV return, chassis and protective earth have different jobs. Continuity is sought **within each permitted return domain**; no rubric item below proposes copper across the safety barrier. His discussion of protective-earth terminology is introductory and should not be treated as an appliance safety standard.

The decoupling discussion is most valuable as a warning against choosing capacitors by a ritual value list. The book's measured example shows supply noise increasing as bypass capacitors are moved farther from IC pins (Fig. 14.4, p. 325 / PDF 326), and its layout figure contrasts long/narrow connection with short/wide local copper (Fig. 14.5, p. 326 / PDF 327). It argues that several identical-value MLCCs can outperform a mixed-value trio when mounting inductance is comparable, and mixed values can introduce antiresonance peaks (pp. 326–335 / PDF 327–336). For Temper, evaluate *effective capacitance at bias and temperature*, ESR/ESL, pulse duty, voltage stress, and the actual loop from source to return. The author's broad 10–22 µF and 2× rail-voltage suggestions are starting examples for ordinary low-voltage digital rails, not exact selections for hot bridge film capacitors, gate supplies, isolation supplies, or safety capacitors (pp. 405–407, 343 / PDF 406–408, 344).

The same specificity applies to BOM simplification. Fewer unique parts can reduce assembly mistakes and inventory effort (pp. 216–217, 397, 408–410 / PDF 217–218, 398, 409–411), but parts with distinct electrical, thermal or safety duties should not be consolidated for convenience. An alternate MPN is qualified only after its fit, ratings, behavior, package and availability are checked. For a power stage, a superficially similar capacitor or switching device may change loss, dv/dt, leakage, surge behavior or temperature margin. That is an inference from the book's part-qualification method, not a particular failure found on stage15b.

## Scoring convention and evidence rule

Use `0` = failed criterion, `1` = serious concern, `2` = design supported by analysis but pending physical verification, `3` = verified margin under stated worst-case conditions, `U` = evidence absent or ambiguous, and `NA` = explicitly justified inapplicability. A clean KiCad DRC may establish a limited geometric constraint; it does not automatically produce a `3` for thermal, EMC, isolation, circuit behavior or manufacturability. For every criterion, retain the exact board hash, assumption set, drawing or calculation, reviewer's name, measurement method and instrument bandwidth. A physical prototype is required for most `3` ratings. **Do not total these scores into a board-readiness number.** Suspicious geometry alone warrants a concern only after its mechanism is identified; score 0 requires a violated applicable requirement. Missing essential inputs remain U. A failed critical criterion remains visible regardless of how many other criteria score well.

The anchors below refine that common convention. `2` always means the named model, document or geometry supports the choice while intended-condition verification is still missing. `3` requires the stated evidence and demonstrated margin; it is unavailable when only a digital board exists. Numerical acceptance limits must come from Temper requirements, exact manufacturer data, selected fab/assembler capability, applicable safety requirements and validated measurements—not from an unconditioned book example.

## Actionable criteria

### Requirements and exact-part decisions

**BP-01 — Define the function and risk before geometry.** *Physical rationale:* a perfect route can faithfully realize the wrong behavior; expensive risks are easiest to change before layout. *Question:* Does each block have an operating envelope, failure consequence and planned proof? *Evidence:* the current plan of record, power tree, operating and fault envelopes, risk register, requirements-to-test matrix. *Anchors:* `0` a documented critical safety/functional requirement is violated; `1` requirement exists but has no bound or proof path; `2` bounds and verification plan are reviewed; `3` measured/qualified performance meets them with margin; `U` requirement record unavailable. *Advice/trade-off:* prioritize irreversible high-energy and isolation choices before cosmetic or cost tuning; a later feature may deserve its own board revision. **Source:** chs. 1, 7–8, pp. 25–37, 177–195 / PDF 26–38, 178–196. **Temper inference:** enumerate bridge operating modes, coil/pan variation, startup, faults and maintenance states rather than copying the author's widget examples.

**BP-02 — Classify nets by the harm their parasitics can cause.** *Rationale:* a net's current, edge rate, victim sensitivity and return geometry determine its layout constraints. *Question:* Are switching, high-current, quiet sensing, communication and isolation-boundary nets assigned distinct constraints and review methods? *Evidence:* net-class table tied to the pinned schematic, rise-time/current estimates, constraint setup and documented exceptions. *Anchors:* `0` a critical net violates a defined class-specific electrical or safety limit; `1` classes listed but no physical checks; `2` class-specific rules and predicted margins; `3` measured waveforms support the margins; `U` signal/current data unavailable. *Advice/trade-off:* useful classes can add routing burden, but they keep the expensive constraints visible. **Source:** pp. 71–84 / PDF 72–85; ch. 4, pp. 94–111 / PDF 95–112. **Temper inference:** bridge switching and sensing are separate review families.

**BP-03 — Qualify each exact MPN for its actual duty.** *Rationale:* pin compatibility and nominal value do not guarantee identical switching, thermal or transient behavior. *Question:* Is every critical part justified with exact manufacturer number, current datasheet revision, operating/fault ratings and qualification evidence? *Evidence:* resolved-components/BOM record, datasheets, derating worksheet, supplier status, samples or eval results. *Anchors:* `0` part cannot meet an identified stress; `1` nominal match without worst-case/footprint proof; `2` exact-part analysis supports use; `3` sample or representative test confirms critical behavior with margin; `U` exact MPN or datasheet missing. *Advice/trade-off:* favor known parts where they satisfy requirements, but do not reuse a familiar part beyond its verified envelope. **Source:** pp. 202–203, 207–217, 395–400 / PDF 203–204, 208–218, 396–401. **Temper inference:** qualify switching devices, bus film capacitors, drivers, shunts, isolation and protection parts separately.

**BP-04 — Verify value under operating bias, heat and tolerance.** *Rationale:* marked capacitor/resistor values can depart materially from in-circuit values. *Question:* Do calculations use effective capacitance, ESR/ESL, resistor tolerance/power/voltage and temperature behavior at Temper conditions? *Evidence:* manufacturer curves, exact dielectric, tolerance stack, stress model and optional bench characterization. *Anchors:* `0` known derating violates needed function; `1` calculation uses nominal value alone; `2` bounded effective values support design; `3` measured assembled circuit meets the bound; `U` manufacturer data absent. *Advice/trade-off:* larger or higher-rated parts cost space; narrower margins may demand multiple parts or a different dielectric. **Source:** pp. 402–407 / PDF 403–408. **Temper inference:** 2× voltage rating is not a general safety classification or surge qualification.

**BP-05 — Make alternatives genuinely interchangeable.** *Rationale:* supply resilience is useful only if each replacement preserves fit and electrical behavior. *Question:* Are approved substitutes compared on package/pinout, stress, loss, lifetime, parasitics and procurement rather than nominal category? *Evidence:* alternate MPN matrix, qualification conditions, cost and lead-time snapshots. *Anchors:* `0` listed alternate is incompatible; `1` alternates named without critical comparison; `2` desk qualification complete; `3` representative assembled substitution tested; `U` source/availability unclear. *Advice/trade-off:* BOM consolidation reduces handling risk, but imposing one part where distinct duties require different performance is false economy. **Source:** pp. 195, 202, 216–217, 397–400, 408–410 / PDF 196, 203, 217–218, 398–401, 409–411.

**BP-06 — Tie symbol, footprint and physical sample to one part.** *Rationale:* a correct circuit fails if pin numbering, polarity, body size or land pattern is wrong. *Question:* Does each new or custom footprint match the exact drawing and a physical sample? *Evidence:* pin mapping, dimensions/tolerances, 1:1 print or sample fit, footprint review, assembly drawing. *Anchors:* `0` known pin/pad mismatch; `1` imported library shape unverified; `2` independent drawing-to-library check and fit assessment; `3` assembled sample and inspection confirm fit/joints; `U` manufacturer drawing/sample unavailable. *Advice/trade-off:* a breakout coupon can cost less than a respin; pad enlargement can ease hand soldering but may change assembly yield/spacing. **Source:** pp. 202–203, 376–378, 408–409 / PDF 203–204, 377–379, 409–410.

**BP-07 — Match package to assembly, repair and force.** *Rationale:* mechanical loads can tear SMD pads; hidden pads complicate inspection and rework. *Question:* Are connectors, studs, heavy/heat-generating parts and fine-pitch devices suited to the planned assembler and service method? *Evidence:* package drawings, load path, assembly capability, access envelope, rework plan. *Anchors:* `0` package or attachment cannot carry expected load; `1` plausible but assembly/service unproven; `2` supplier and mechanical review support choice; `3` assembly trial and stress inspection pass; `U` process/load unspecified. *Advice/trade-off:* through-hole joints provide mechanical strength but consume board area and routing channels; SMD simplifies automation. **Source:** pp. 383–388, 410–411 / PDF 384–389, 411–412.

### Conductors and current loops

**BP-08 — Size every current path from worst-case heating and loss.** *Rationale:* copper temperature rise is set by `I²R` and local thermal resistance, which differs by layer, nearby copper and enclosure. *Question:* Do steady, startup and fault-limited currents fit each path's minimum cross section and allowed temperature/drop? *Evidence:* copper stackup, path width/length including necks, resistance and thermal estimates, planned thermography/current test. *Anchors:* `0` predicted or measured over-temperature/drop; `1` uses a generic ampacity chart without local assumptions; `2` path-specific worst-case estimate supports margin; `3` representative board tested at bounded ambient and load; `U` current or copper spec unknown. *Advice/trade-off:* more copper or layers cost money and can complicate spacing; widening in available area is often cheaper. **Source:** pp. 121–133, 143–165 / PDF 122–134, 144–166. **Limit:** the book's 6 mil/1 A and 20 mil/3 A examples are not Temper limits.

**BP-09 — Treat via and pad transitions as path bottlenecks.** *Rationale:* a wide copper path can be throttled by one via barrel, narrow pad throat or connector landing. *Question:* Does the complete route, including transitions and parallel-current sharing, meet current and temperature needs? *Evidence:* route cross-section map, finished-hole/plating tolerances, via count and placement, resistance/thermal calculation, prototype temperature result. *Anchors:* `0` known weak transition exceeds its limit; `1` wide pours assumed sufficient despite unassessed necks; `2` all constrictions modeled; `3` worst-case board measurement confirms margin; `U` plating or geometry unknown. *Advice/trade-off:* multiple vias reduce resistance and spread heat but consume clearance and can create return-plane perforation. **Source:** pp. 132–133, 167–174, 463–464 / PDF 133–134, 168–175, 464–465.

**BP-10 — Calculate DC drop and sense error along the real return path.** *Rationale:* milliohms become meaningful at high current and local reference shifts can corrupt protection thresholds. *Question:* Are conductor, connector, shunt and return drops allocated against the device and measurement budgets? *Evidence:* physical path lengths, copper/temperature model, connector/contact resistance, Kelvin topology and tolerance analysis. *Anchors:* `0` drop or reference error exceeds required margin; `1` only schematic ideal nodes analyzed; `2` complete path budget supports margin; `3` measured differential potentials under load agree; `U` return geometry/limits unknown. *Advice/trade-off:* widening/shortening routes saves heat and accuracy, but long, quiet sense routes may be needed to escape the power region. **Source:** pp. 148–174 / PDF 149–175. **Temper inference:** analyze `OCP_KELVIN_P/N`, `LEG_RET`, `HV_RET` according to the pinned source's stated roles, without assuming they are interchangeable grounds.

**BP-11 — Trace each fast power loop and its return.** *Rationale:* loop inductance rises with length and separation and falls with broad, closely coupled paths. Voltage overshoot is related to `L·di/dt`. *Question:* Can the bridge commutation, local bus, snubber and gate-drive loops be drawn as closed paths with no unexplained detour? *Evidence:* highlighted top/bottom copper and component pads, estimated parasitic model, switching-waveform plan. *Anchors:* `0` forced wide loop or absent return compromises a stated limit; `1` loop inferred from schematic only; `2` closed physical loops mapped and estimated; `3` overshoot/ringing/temperature measured with margin; `U` current waveform or board geometry unavailable. *Advice/trade-off:* close-coupled routes can increase capacitance or reduce spacing; those costs need explicit evaluation. **Source:** pp. 94–109, 269–281, 320–326 / PDF 95–110, 270–282, 321–327. **Temper inference:** apply to the full bridge, not the book's LED demo.

**BP-12 — Keep sensitive return currents out of shared narrow segments.** *Rationale:* two loops sharing a narrow impedance couple by its voltage drop and mutual inductance. *Question:* Do sensing, control and gate reference paths share copper with bridge current or each other where that creates a harmful reference shift? *Evidence:* current-flow overlay per operating mode, return-path impedance estimate, sensitivity budget. *Anchors:* `0` shared-return voltage violates a defined victim noise or threshold budget; `1` return joining point unexamined; `2` sharing limited and analyzed; `3` noise at victim input measured under worst switching; `U` current paths not established. *Advice/trade-off:* separate local paths can reduce coupling but excessive splitting can create a worse gap; connect according to the intended domain topology. **Source:** pp. 274–287 / PDF 275–288; Fig. 12.7, p. 280 / PDF 281.

**BP-13 — Preserve a nearby uninterrupted return for fast signals.** *Rationale:* high-frequency plane current follows the trace; a gap diverts it, increases loop area and couples neighboring nets. *Question:* Does every fast or noise-sensitive signal retain an adjacent permitted-domain return through layer changes and board regions? *Evidence:* stackup plus copper overlay, gap/cutout crossing report, signal/return via pairs where warranted. *Anchors:* `0` a return break causes a defined signal-integrity or emission limit to be violated; `1` gap treatment unproven; `2` return continuity shown in geometry; `3` signal integrity/EMI measurement confirms margin; `U` stackup or route unknown. *Advice/trade-off:* a longer signal over a continuous return may beat a shorter route over a split; isolation cuts remain mandatory and signals crossing domains need an appropriate isolation component. **Source:** pp. 279–292, 443–449, 478–482 / PDF 280–293, 444–450, 479–483.

**BP-14 — Review layer changes as two coupled transitions.** *Rationale:* the signal via changes conductor layer and its return must transfer too. *Question:* At each critical transition, is the nearby return path explicit and electrically legal? *Evidence:* via-pair map, adjacent plane identity, cutout geometry and edge-rate analysis. *Anchors:* `0` transition has no legal low-impedance return and violates noise budget; `1` return assumed to find a remote route; `2` local return transfer analyzed; `3` measured waveform/EMI behavior confirms it; `U` via/plane assignment unavailable. *Advice/trade-off:* an adjacent return via costs placement area but can avoid cavity excitation; two ground planes must be the same electrical domain before stitching. **Source:** pp. 467, 478–482 / PDF 468, 479–483. The book's GHz cavity example is conditional on geometry and rise time.

**BP-15 — Keep unrelated fast aggressors away from quiet victims.** *Rationale:* long, close parallel runs and interrupted returns raise capacitive and inductive coupling. *Question:* Are low-level sensing and fault inputs separated from switching nodes and do they have controlled return paths? *Evidence:* aggressor-victim route map, coupling estimate or simulation, victim noise limit, planned worst-case capture. *Anchors:* `0` coupling violates a defined threshold; `1` adjacency accepted without estimate; `2` spacing/return geometry and estimate support margin; `3` victim waveform meets threshold under worst switching; `U` sensitivity/edge data missing. *Advice/trade-off:* extra spacing consumes board area; a continuous adjacent return may matter more than forcing distance alone. **Source:** pp. 102–108, 271–274, 290–292 / PDF 103–109, 272–275, 291–293.

### Power integrity and layout choices

**BP-16 — Place local bypass by total loop, not centroid distance.** *Rationale:* pin-to-capacitor-to-return inductance governs fast rail sag and ground bounce. *Question:* Is each driver/control device's effective bypass loop short, broad and free of narrow shared returns? *Evidence:* both sides of every local supply loop, capacitor placement and via map, expected transient current, schematic value. *Anchors:* `0` the installed bypass path cannot meet a defined device-pin rail limit; `1` close-looking placement with unexamined return; `2` full loop and effective capacitance analyzed; `3` rail waveform at the device pin confirms margin; `U` return route or transient load unknown. *Advice/trade-off:* tiny packages help only if their connections do; keep series diagnostic jumpers outside the local bypass loop. **Source:** pp. 302–309, 323–326, 335–336, 358 / PDF 303–310, 324–327, 336–337, 359; Figs. 14.4–14.5.

**BP-17 — Select decoupling as an impedance network.** *Rationale:* real MLCCs have ESL, ESR, DC-bias loss and potential parallel resonance. *Question:* Does the selected set meet rail transient demand over the relevant frequency range without unexplained antiresonance? *Evidence:* exact-part effective-C curves, mounted path estimates, impedance or transient model, rail capture. *Anchors:* `0` known impedance peak causes failure; `1` mixed values chosen by habit alone; `2` network model supports margin; `3` measured pin rail agrees under load; `U` model or operating spectrum absent. *Advice/trade-off:* identical-value parts simplify BOM but need sufficient effective charge; deliberately mixed technologies may be right if damping and load behavior are shown. **Source:** pp. 326–335, 405–407, 465 / PDF 327–336, 406–408, 466.

**BP-18 — Separate source filtering from local transient supply.** *Rationale:* a filter at the regulator limits its own output noise, while a nearby capacitor supplies fast load current; they solve different mechanisms. *Question:* Are source ripple, load self-noise and sensitive-rail ingress addressed at the correct locations? *Evidence:* rail-noise budget, VRM data, filter topology/placement, measured or simulated transfer and startup behavior. *Anchors:* `0` rail violates its limit; `1` capacitors/beads placed without mechanism; `2` source and load effects analyzed; `3` rail and sensitive input measured with margin; `U` noise limits unknown. *Advice/trade-off:* ferrites can isolate a sensitive rail but add DC loss, resonance and startup behavior; never insert return impedance casually. **Source:** pp. 316–320, 336–343 / PDF 317–321, 337–344.

**BP-19 — Evaluate copper pours by function and boundary.** *Rationale:* a floating pour may resonate and raise coupling; a power pour may hide a neck; purposeful copper can conduct current or heat. *Question:* Is every pour identified as return, power, heat spreader or manufacturing copper, with a continuous path and no orphaned islands? *Evidence:* filled-zone plot, connection and neck map, thermal/current calculation, fab feedback. *Anchors:* `0` pour shorts a boundary or creates a known harmful path; `1` indiscriminate pour assumed beneficial; `2` function and parasitics reviewed; `3` physical thermal/noise behavior confirms margin; `U` filled geometry unavailable. *Advice/trade-off:* keep a purposeful high-current copper shape when justified even though Bogatin discourages decorative signal-layer fill. His Fig. 22.2 simulation uses two 10 mil traces at 150 mil separation and GHz frequencies; it does not prove every Temper pour harmful. **Source:** pp. 461–464 / PDF 462–465, Fig. 22.2 p. 463 / PDF 464.

**BP-20 — Match stackup to routes, returns, voltage domains and fab.** *Rationale:* layer choice sets conductor capacity, return proximity, dielectric spacing, cost and routing density. *Question:* Does the chosen stackup provide legal, robust reference and power paths for every domain while meeting fabrication and isolation needs? *Evidence:* signed stackup, dielectric/copper tolerances, layer use, return map, fabricator quote. *Anchors:* `0` stackup cannot meet a required spacing/current/return; `1` copied template without analysis; `2` trade study supports it; `3` fabricated cross-section and electrical tests confirm margins; `U` fab stackup uncommitted. *Advice/trade-off:* Bogatin prefers two internal ground planes for an ordinary four-layer signal board; Temper may require a different allocation for high current or isolation. **Source:** pp. 473–482 / PDF 474–483.

**BP-21 — Protect solderability without sacrificing electrical purpose.** *Rationale:* a massive copper heat sink at a pad can defeat reflow or hand repair; thermal relief increases thermal and electrical impedance. *Question:* Are pad-to-plane connections chosen for the joint process and the path's current/heat duty? *Evidence:* pad/zone connection style, assembler capability, thermal profile, joint inspection, path loss model. *Anchors:* `0` joint or path fails; `1` thermal relief applied mechanically without purpose; `2` process and electrical trade-off reviewed; `3` representative assembly and temperature test pass; `U` assembly process unknown. *Advice/trade-off:* a thermal via is intended to move heat; a thermal-relief connection limits heat during soldering. Do not confuse them. **Source:** pp. 45–48, 433–436 / PDF 46–49, 434–437.

**BP-22 — Review board-edge placement and physical service access.** *Rationale:* connector entry, probe access and external loads constrain routing and assembly before traces are drawn. *Question:* Are external connectors, test interfaces, heat-producing devices and mechanically constrained parts located and oriented for their real enclosure and assembly sequence? *Evidence:* board outline, connector mating envelope, keepouts, 3D fit, assembly steps. *Anchors:* `0` collision/unmated connector or inaccessible required operation; `1` fit assumed from 2D footprint; `2` mechanical and assembly drawings support fit; `3` physical mockup confirms it; `U` enclosure/interface not pinned. *Advice/trade-off:* convenient probe access can add length/area; place high-energy loops and required isolation first. **Source:** pp. 439–443 / PDF 440–444. **Temper limit:** the enclosure snapshot may not match stage15b, per `hardware/README.md`.

**BP-23 — Verify DRC, schematic intent and artwork independently.** *Rationale:* ERC catches some wiring errors, DRC catches encoded geometry, and DFM catches selected process limits; none understands all functional intent. *Question:* Has a reviewer checked the current schematic, constraints, filled copper and generated outputs against the intended circuit? *Evidence:* ERC/DRC reports, rule coverage, independent review notes, Gerber/drill viewer and manufacturer DFM response. *Anchors:* `0` unresolved critical discrepancy; `1` only tool green status offered; `2` independent cross-check and output inspection complete; `3` fabricated board agrees with release data and passes electrical inspection; `U` rule/output identity ambiguous. *Advice/trade-off:* a second review costs time before order but saves respin risk. **Source:** pp. 373–381, 423–424, 455–457, 485–488 / PDF 374–382, 424–425, 456–458, 486–489.

**BP-24 — Provide intentional probe points and references.** *Rationale:* measurement lead loops and unknown references can create false ringing or hazardous probing. *Question:* Can every planned bring-up quantity be reached with the required bandwidth, return and isolation method? *Evidence:* test-point list with net/domain and expected range, probe geometry, instrument isolation and test plan. *Anchors:* `0` essential node cannot be measured safely or meaningfully; `1` ad hoc probing only; `2` measurement geometry and procedure documented; `3` repeatable bench capture with known probe setup; `U` test plan absent. *Advice/trade-off:* extra test hardware takes space and can perturb high-speed loops; locate it outside the critical current path. **Source:** pp. 355–369 / PDF 356–370, especially §15.7 pp. 361–365 / PDF 362–366. **Temper inference:** instrument safety needs a separate qualified procedure.

**BP-25 — Make staged bring-up and fault isolation possible.** *Rationale:* incremental assembly and power-domain isolation reduce the search space for a failure. *Question:* Can the board be checked bare, then energized by controlled stages with measurable stop conditions? *Evidence:* sequence, continuity/short checks, current limits, isolation options, expected waveform and fault table. *Anchors:* `0` first power step lacks a safe bound or required fault stop; `1` full assembly/energization planned without intermediate checks; `2` reviewed sequence and fixtures; `3` executed sequence with recorded results; `U` procedure not written. *Advice/trade-off:* jumpers and indicators help diagnosis but may insert inductance, leakage or unsafe accessible conductors. **Source:** pp. 347–369, 491–508 / PDF 348–370, 492–509.

**BP-26 — Define what a pass means and retain the evidence.** *Rationale:* a board that powers on has only passed an initial smoke test; characterization, stress and margin follow. *Question:* Does each test state a stimulus, expected bound, instrument, setup, observed result and disposition? *Evidence:* test matrix, calibrated waveforms, thermal images, deviations and root-cause log tied to board identity. *Anchors:* `0` demonstrated failure against a requirement; `1` pass based on unbounded observation; `2` tests and limits defined but not run; `3` repeatable results show margin across the required envelope; `U` records missing. *Advice/trade-off:* full characterization costs time; focus earliest on high-consequence unknowns. **Source:** pp. 185–187, 351–353, 491–495, 515–516 / PDF 186–188, 352–354, 492–496, 516–517.

**BP-27 — Keep release identity and fab process explicit.** *Rationale:* mismatched drawings, drill, BOM or pick-and-place files can manufacture a design other than the reviewed one. *Question:* Does one release manifest tie schematic, board, generated manufacturing data, exact BOM and assembly instructions to an immutable revision? *Evidence:* file hashes, fab stackup and capability acceptance, checked Gerber/NC drill, BOM/PnP consistency and revision marking. *Anchors:* `0` conflicting or missing critical release file; `1` generated files uninspected or unversioned; `2` package cross-check and vendor review complete; `3` first-article inspection matches the manifest; `U` release package absent. *Advice/trade-off:* vendor defaults are economical when they meet constraints; special copper, finish, holes or tolerances need a specific performance benefit. **Source:** pp. 183–185, 485–488 / PDF 184–186, 486–489. The 2021 book's quoted vendor prices/capabilities are historical examples.

**BP-28 — Review domain separation independently of return continuity.** *Rationale:* fast-current loops favor nearby returns, while mains-to-SELV separation forbids conductive shortcuts and imposes insulation geometry. *Question:* Are hot, SELV, chassis and protective-earth domains identified, and are all intentional crossings rated components with sufficient physical separation for the eventual product requirements? *Evidence:* domain map, barrier/cutout geometry, exact isolation component approvals, applicable standards analysis and independent safety review. *Anchors:* `0` unintended conductive crossing or known insufficient barrier; `1` continuity advice applied without isolation analysis; `2` requirements and geometry support separation; `3` qualified hardware/process and safety tests confirm it; `U` standards, construction or barrier data absent. *Advice/trade-off:* do not stitch a plane merely to improve return flow across a required barrier; use an appropriate isolator and route each side's return locally. **Source boundary:** Bogatin's pp. 287–292, 465–467 / PDF 288–293, 466–468 explain return continuity but provide no appliance isolation design method. This criterion is a Temper-specific inference requiring an independent safety source.

## Decision log for eventual Temper application

1. **Adopt the physical-loop method.** Start from operating and fault currents, identify both outgoing and return conductors, then assign a voltage, temperature and noise budget. This is directly supported by Bogatin's chs. 4–6 and 12–14. Whether stage15b satisfies it remains unassessed.
2. **Use the book's concrete numbers only as annotated examples.** Its 6/20 mil widths, 13 mil via, 10–22 µF caps, 2× capacitor rating, 100 mm panel economics, and two-ground-plane preference were developed for particular prototype conditions and vendors. Recompute or replace them from stage15b requirements and current supplier data before any score.
3. **Preserve isolation domain boundaries.** A continuous return within the hot domain or within SELV may be valuable. Any cross-domain connection must be the intended rated isolation element. The book's ordinary ground-plane examples do not authorize crossing the barrier.
4. **Allow purposeful power copper and mixed capacitor technologies.** Bogatin's critique targets arbitrary pours and value rituals. A bus or bridge copper polygon may be necessary for current/heat; film, electrolytic and MLCC parts may serve different pulse, damping, bulk and high-frequency roles. Demand evidence for each.
5. **Treat a green rule check as one receipt.** Add schematic-intent, exact-part, fab-artwork, loop/thermal, measurement and safety reviews. Bogatin explicitly distinguishes tool checks from functional performance and prototype qualification.

## Recommended sequence when the board is reviewed

First, lock the manifest and board hash with the hardware baseline guard. Build a net-class and stress table from the pinned schematic and exact parts. Second, draw the high-energy and measurement loops directly on current copper views; calculate hot resistance, transition bottlenecks and expected `L·di/dt` noise under bounded cases. Third, check local bypass and return continuity, then compare noise estimates with the selected rail and sensing limits. Fourth, assess footprint, solderability, fab stackup, release outputs and mechanical access with the intended suppliers. Finally, define a staged low-energy-to-operating test sequence with safe instrumentation and explicit stop criteria; score `3` only after a representative build and measurements. Preserve per-item `U` where coil/pan measurements, part qualification, enclosure revision or fab process are still unresolved. This sequence prepares a review; it does not authorize energizing the current candidate.
