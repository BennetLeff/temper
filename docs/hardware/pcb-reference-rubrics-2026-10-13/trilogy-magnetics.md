## Rating method

Apply each criterion to a defined revision and operating envelope. Record **0** for a demonstrated failure of the agreed requirement, **1** for a serious concern with credible evidence, **2** for a supported design whose critical validation is still pending, **3** for verified margin under representative worst cases, **U** for unknown because an essential input or measurement is missing, or **NA** with a documented reason. **U and NA take precedence over numeric scoring** when applicable: do not turn an unmeasured topology, unknown impedance, missing datasheet, or guessed emission mode into a 0 or 1. A plausible layout concern is 1 only if the geometry is observed and a credible failure mechanism is identified; 0 requires an actual requirement violation shown by analysis or measurement. A schematic, a simulation, and a bench measurement are different evidence levels. Do not average scores into a readiness verdict; any unresolved safety or stress item can dominate the outcome. Establish the numeric requirement from Temper's rated input, emissions/immunity targets, component datasheets, and test plan before assigning a pass. ## Detailed rubric

### A. Characterize the intended magnetic function

**MAG-01 — Identify the job of every magnetic element.** Map each part to energy storage, differential filtering, common mode filtering, isolation, current sensing, or the external induction coil. Ask what currents actually traverse each winding and where they return. Evidence: a mode-labeled schematic and a current-path drawing for normal operation, start, trip, and faults. A concern is calling a common mode choke a generic series inductor or treating the pan coil as a catalog buck inductor; failure is a path that prevents the intended magnetic cancellation or measurement. The book distinguishes lossy EMC ferrites from high-Q energy-storage inductors (printed p. 53, PDF p. 54) and explains compensated winding currents (p. 329, PDF p. 330). **Temper inference:** separate mains L1, tank coil, and T1 review tracks.

**MAG-02 — Derive an operating envelope before choosing catalog curves.** List line voltage/frequency, bridge frequency and harmonics, worst-case pan material/position, load power, tank current waveform, transient and fault peaks, ambient temperature, and available cooling. Evidence: measured or justified extrema and duty/time distributions. A nominal-only dataset is a concern; applying rated current without thermal and peak context is a failure if it masks overstress; unknown waveforms are U. Core loss depends on flux waveform, dc bias, harmonics, volt-seconds, and temperature, and the ordinary sinusoidal Steinmetz fit can be very wrong for pulse drive (printed pp. 25–29, PDF pp. 26–30). **Temper inference:** use coil/pan characterization rather than the provisional 70 µH number.

**MAG-03 — Distinguish the interference mode and frequency.** For each troublesome conducted or radiated band, establish common mode versus differential mode using appropriate probes and return paths. Evidence: mode-separated spectra and current paths at line, chassis/PE, coil cable, and low-voltage ports. A single aggregate EMI trace with no defensible mode diagnosis is U; a guessed cure receives 0 only when measured results show violation of an applicable requirement. Source: common/differential definitions and compensated choke behavior (printed p. 329, PDF p. 330), filter source/sink discussion (pp. 87–89, PDF pp. 88–90), and mains-cable high-frequency behavior (p. 627, PDF p. 628). **Trade-off:** a change can reduce one mode while transferring noise into another.

**MAG-04 — Define the useful signal band that filtering must preserve.** Before specifying a filter, identify wanted line power, sensing bandwidth, controller rail transients, and resonant-tank behavior. Evidence: passband and load-transient requirements and measured waveforms before and after filtering. A quiet EMC plot with degraded control or heating performance is a failure, not a win. The book says filter effectiveness depends on the useful band and on the source and sink impedances (printed pp. 86–94, PDF pp. 87–95). **Temper inference:** tank-current sensing and protective trips have different passbands from a DC bias supply; qualify each separately.

### B. Select and derate exact magnetic parts

**MAG-05 — Separate thermal current rating from saturation current.** For every inductor and choke collect the maker's test conditions for `Irated`, `Isat`, permissible temperature rise, inductance-drop definition, and operating temperature. Evidence: current waveform and hot-case temperature with exact MPN data. A catalog current number without its definition is U; margin only at room temperature is a concern. The source notes thermal rated current may be higher or lower than saturation current, and saturation definitions vary from 10% to 40% inductance loss (printed p. 52, PDF p. 53). **Trade-off:** a physically larger core can improve saturation and thermal margin but cost area and increase parasitic capacitance.

**MAG-06 — Check bias-dependent impedance and inductance.** Use curves or test data at the actual dc, low-frequency, and surge bias, not merely 0 A impedance at 100 MHz. Evidence: `Z(f,I,T)` or `L(I,T)` data, including transient peak. Missing bias data is U; measured bias degradation is 0 only if it violates the defined attenuation or inductance requirement. Ferrite-bead current limits, high-temperature derating, and DC-bias effects are discussed at printed pp. 289–291 (PDF pp. 290–292); core B-H nonlinearity and saturation at pp. 23–33 and p. 52 (PDF pp. 24–34, 53). **Temper inference:** line current should largely cancel in a CMC, while imbalance, leakage flux, startup, and faults deserve their own check.

**MAG-07 — Treat AC copper loss as distinct from DCR.** Record winding DCR and impedance or resistance at the operating spectrum; estimate `I_rms² R_ac(f)` over harmonics, then validate with temperature. Evidence: current spectrum, hot resistance or calorimetric/temperature data, and thermal model. An `I²DCR` estimate alone is a concern where high-frequency current is substantial. The book details skin/proximity effects and multilayer winding geometry (printed pp. 41–49, PDF pp. 42–50); its copper examples are demonstrations, not a universal correction factor. **Trade-off:** larger copper may lower DCR while proximity effects, winding arrangement, cost, and space still govern AC loss.

**MAG-08 — Account for core loss with the real flux waveform.** Derive volt-seconds and flux swing for each core at worst voltage/frequency; use current manufacturer loss data or calibrated models, and compare to measured heating. Evidence: flux estimate, waveform, core material, temperature, and measured in-system rise. A sine-wave core-loss curve used uncritically for a bridge or pulsed supply is a concern. The book's modified/generalized Steinmetz discussion explicitly lists limitations (printed pp. 25–29, PDF pp. 26–30). **Trade-off:** operating a core at lower flux often means more turns or volume, affecting copper loss and leakage.

**MAG-09 — Confirm thermal aging and neighboring heat.** Test long enough for thermal equilibrium and account for heat from nearby bridge devices, capacitors, enclosure, and pan. Evidence: thermocouple/IR map with emissivity control, ambient extremes, component temperature ratings and life basis. A cool open-bench part with no enclosure extrapolation is U or 1. The book warns self-heating must include heat coupling from other components (printed p. 28, PDF p. 29) and discusses thermal derating and material aging (p. 371, PDF p. 372). **Temper inference:** use present part data; the vendor's example 125 °C/200 °C materials do not set Temper limits.

**MAG-10 — Check parasitic capacitance and self resonance.** Measure or obtain `Z(f)` and phase across the relevant spectrum, including cable and board parasitics. Evidence: impedance sweep or manufacturer S-parameters plus installed measurement. An unsupported claim that the part stays inductive above its self resonance is a concern; 0 requires a demonstrated violation of the required installed impedance or phase. The real-inductor model shows winding capacitance and capacitive behavior above self resonance (printed p. 41, PDF p. 42). **Trade-off:** more turns raise low-frequency impedance but can reduce high-frequency benefit through capacitance.

**MAG-11 — Verify insulation, voltage, impulse, and mechanical clearances independently.** For mains chokes, sensing transformers, and any magnetic crossing domains, gather exact-part insulation system, test voltage, working voltage, creepage/clearance, pin mapping, and applicable end-product requirements. Evidence: datasheet and certification documents, drawing, and board-to-enclosure spacing checks. Missing insulation basis is U and must not be promoted by a magnetic-performance score. The book's transformer section states isolation is an application function (printed p. 130, PDF p. 131), and its catalog high-voltage examples contain package-specific claims (e.g. p. 380, PDF p. 381). **Temper inference:** current T1 placement on the switch node makes its isolation and common mode coupling review important; book values do not certify it.

**MAG-12 — Maintain a current exact-part qualification record.** Record manufacturer and MPN, revision/date of datasheet, winding/pin orientation, dimensions/footprint, availability and second-source equivalence, thermal and surge ratings, and measured deviations from the model. Evidence: linked manufacturer documents and controlled BOM. An obsolete book order code without exact-part qualification is U; a documented qualification with a credible unresolved discrepancy is 1. The component chapters (Part II, printed pp. 276–475) are excellent examples of *which columns* matter but their listed Würth families and prices/stock are time-dependent. **Trade-off:** a nominally interchangeable choke may differ in impedance versus frequency, coupling, hot DCR, orientation, and insulation.

### C. Mains, EMI filters, and physical layout

**MAG-13 — Model the installed filter with actual terminations.** Determine source and load impedances by measurement or bounded models over the targeted noise band; simulate and then measure insertion loss in the actual circuit. Evidence: both unfiltered and filtered spectra and `Zsource(f)`, `Zload(f)` assumptions. A 50 Ω catalog insertion-loss trace alone is U, not a pass. The book's example changes by about 30 dB when terminations change (printed pp. 83–86, PDF pp. 84–87); these are example results, not Temper attenuation. **Trade-off:** a T filter is less sensitive to some low-impedance terminations but adds parts, loss, space, and cost (p. 86, PDF p. 87).

**MAG-14 — Examine the whole filter's resonance and damping.** Include CMC leakage inductance, X/Y capacitors, line/source inductance, parasitic resistance, and converter load behavior. Evidence: impedance sweep or small-signal model plus transient/startup test. A sharp impedance peak overlapping control or conducted-emission bands is a concern until damped; observed instability is 0. The book describes LC resonance, damping, and source/load dependence (printed pp. 92–95, PDF pp. 93–96). It also warns converter input-filter impedance may interact with negative incremental input resistance (pp. 188–189, PDF pp. 189–190). **Temper inference:** the latter textbook derivation is for switching regulators; derive the actual front-end dynamics before transplanting it to an unfiltered rectified bus and resonant bridge.

**MAG-15 — Keep common and differential behavior separate for L1.** Obtain CMC common-mode impedance versus frequency, differential leakage inductance, winding balance, DCR, phase, and attenuation under line-current stress. Evidence: exact TDK curves and mounted tests in both modes. A large common-mode inductance number does not establish differential attenuation. Current compensation cancels desired opposing line currents in the core (printed p. 329, PDF p. 330); leakage inductance may supply useful differential impedance in some constructions (printed p. 344, PDF p. 345), but its value and heating are part-specific. **Trade-off:** higher leakage may help one mode but increase voltage stress, resonance, and coupling elsewhere.

**MAG-16 — Place the filter at the boundary it protects.** Trace the noisy path from mains entry to bridge and the return via PE/chassis; evaluate whether unfiltered and filtered sections have a physical bypass through adjacent copper, planes, fasteners, or wires. Evidence: board layers and enclosure/cable routing drawing, plus near-field/port measurements. Observed close geometry with a credible bypass mechanism is 1; 0 needs measured or derived violation of an applicable port requirement. The book's Fig. 1.82 and Fig. 3.118 emphasize source/load separation and entry-point placement (printed pp. 96–98 and 627–628; PDF pp. 97–99 and 628–629). **Temper inference:** board-only evidence cannot settle chassis and inlet geometry.

**MAG-17 — Keep shunt-capacitor return paths short and intentional.** For capacitors used as RF returns, identify exactly which reference plane/chassis node accepts noise current and the closed loop to its source. Evidence: layout cross section, via count/placement, chassis bond impedance, and mode measurements. A capacitor attached by a long branch or uncertain PE/chassis reference is a concern. Fig. 1.82 illustrates a filter bypass and long return; Fig. 3.12/3.13 shows coupling through a shared return (printed pp. 96–98, 486–487; PDF pp. 97–99, 487–488). **Trade-off:** reducing high-frequency impedance can divert noise into a sensitive reference if the return is wrong.

**MAG-18 — Measure parasitic input/output coupling around filter parts.** Inspect overlap, proximity, orientation, winding stray field, and layer transitions around L1 and any local bead/inductor. Evidence: top/bottom layer views, proximity scan, and a small physical A/B change when needed. A plausible unfiltered-to-filtered coupling path is 1 when observed; 0 requires the installed attenuation to miss its requirement or a directly demonstrated bypass. Fig. 3.11 and the placement discussion attribute filter degradation to capacitive/inductive mutual coupling (printed pp. 482–487, PDF pp. 483–488). **Trade-off:** spacing and shielding consume volume and may complicate other high-current paths; no universal clearance distance is given by the book.

**MAG-19 — Validate X/Y capacitor and PE interaction as a system.** Check exact safety-capacitor type, capacitance tolerance, leakage/touch-current budget, discharge behavior, PE bond geometry, and resulting common-mode currents. Evidence: current standards and supplier approvals, electrical measurements in normal/fault conditions, and the full mechanical assembly. The book's mains example uses capacitors to PE and stresses short HF connections (printed pp. 627–629, PDF pp. 628–630). Its suggested values and protective-line choke arrangement are illustrative legacy content, not Temper specifications. **Trade-off:** more Y capacitance may improve emissions while increasing leakage current; preserve protective-earth integrity as a separate gate.

**MAG-20 — Match ferrite suppression to the actual spectrum.** If a bead or cable ferrite is proposed, compare its resistive and reactive impedance at each troublesome harmonic, under current and temperature, and test it in circuit. Evidence: measured spectrum, vendor `R/X/Z` curves, bias/temperature derating, and before/after data. Selecting by one “Ω at 100 MHz” number is a concern. The book distinguishes the absorbing region of ferrites, discusses bead DCR and bias, and cautions that parasitic coupling limits the useful upper band (printed pp. 287–295, 634; PDF pp. 288–296, 635). **Trade-off:** higher nominal impedance often comes with DCR, current, temperature, cost, or useful-signal penalties.

**MAG-21 — Do not use product-family claims as immunity evidence.** The book presents MnZn, NiZn, nanocrystalline, and combined-core CMC families with different frequency bands (printed pp. 336–344, PDF pp. 337–345). For Temper, identify the observed emission and immunity bands first, then compare **measured installed** performance across candidate cores. Evidence: mode-specific spectra and exact-part curves; a broad vendor frequency claim without real source/termination conditions is U. **Trade-off:** high permeability can produce large low-frequency CM impedance but parasitics, core loss, current handling, and package construction can reverse the ranking at higher frequency.

### D. Tank coil, transformer, and sensing

**MAG-22 — Characterize the external induction coil with representative pans.** Measure complex impedance, inductance and effective series resistance over the intended frequency/current/temperature range with no pan, multiple pan sizes/materials, offset pan, and hot conditions. Evidence: controlled sweep and uncertainty, current and voltage waveform, fixture geometry. A single small-signal 70 µH reading is U for tank design. The book connects coil Q, geometry, coupling and resonant capacitance in its inductive-power section (printed pp. 242–247, PDF pp. 243–248), but its Qi examples have different power-transfer objectives and topology. **Temper inference:** pan loading and coil temperature define the actual bounds.

**MAG-23 — Derive tank resonance, circulating current, and device stress from that characterization.** Use the actual series-tank topology and frequency window; include capacitor tolerance, ESR/ESL, coil AC resistance, bus voltage, pan coupling, startup, no-pan, and protection response. Evidence: circuit model compared with voltage/current measurements. A nominal resonant-frequency calculation alone earns at most 2; operation that violates component stress is 0. The source's filter and resonant-circuit fundamentals (printed pp. 55–65 and 203–204, PDF pp. 56–66 and 204–205) motivate the method. Its LLC transformer or wireless-charger equations are not directly applicable to Temper's full-bridge induction load.

**MAG-24 — Optimize coil conductor for AC loss, not DCR alone.** Compare conductor strand/foil/winding geometry at the operating current spectrum and account for proximity to adjacent turns, pan, ferrite, shield, and metalwork. Evidence: hot AC resistance, thermography, and model/measurement correlation. A room-temperature ohmmeter reading alone is U for loss. The book shows skin and proximity effects, and a litz-wire benefit in a wireless-power example (printed pp. 42–49, 245; PDF pp. 43–50, 246). **Trade-off:** litz or wider copper can cut AC loss while changing space, termination, manufacturing yield, stray capacitance, and thermal paths. No strand size can be selected from the book alone.

**MAG-25 — Measure T1 burden, magnetizing error, and frequency response.** For the CST3015-100ED current transformer, derive expected secondary voltage and core flux under the full tank waveform and burden, and measure amplitude, phase, droop, and saturation from lowest valid current through fault peaks. Evidence: exact datasheet plus calibrated primary/secondary waveform comparison. A turns ratio alone is U for protection accuracy. The book gives burden-ratio and magnetizing-current relations (printed pp. 205–206, PDF pp. 206–207). **Trade-off:** greater burden improves signal amplitude but can raise flux/voltage stress and reduce measurement fidelity.

**MAG-26 — Confirm transformer reset and fault behavior.** Establish whether the current transformer's waveform is symmetric enough to reset and how open burden, disconnected secondary, asymmetric drive, and trip sequences affect core state and insulation. Evidence: fault analysis and safe instrumented tests or bounded simulation with exact core/part limits. The source describes reset methods for unipolar CT pulses (printed p. 206, PDF p. 207); it does not mandate one for Temper's tank CT. A missing open-secondary overvoltage assessment is U; demonstrated destructive voltage is 0. **Trade-off:** clamps protect sensing hardware but alter amplitude, phase, and trip thresholds.

**MAG-27 — Quantify transformer leakage versus interwinding capacitance.** Where isolation or sensing transformer choice/placement changes, capture leakage inductance, interwinding capacitance, winding capacitance, DCR, and their effect on high-frequency common-mode current and signal fidelity. Evidence: exact-part measurements or datasheet plus in-circuit validation. The book's Fig. 1.105 and measurement sequence show that interleaving lowers leakage while usually raising coupling capacitance (printed pp. 119–127, PDF pp. 120–128). **Trade-off:** demanding minimum leakage alone can increase noise across an isolation boundary. For Temper T1, analyze the actual construction; the example's numbers are unrelated.

**MAG-28 — Map stray field to sensitive functions and metalwork.** Identify magnetic field sources and receivers at the operating fundamental and edges: tank coil, CMC, CT, current sense conductors, control wiring, chassis, cookware, and ferrite/shield. Evidence: near-field survey and mechanical geometry with temperature effects. A control path that changes with pan position or coil current is a concern until bounded. The source explains coupling and shield/material effects (printed pp. 482–486, 243–248; PDF pp. 483–487, 244–249). **Trade-off:** magnetic shielding can reduce pickup but increase core loss, stray capacitance, cost, or local heating.

**MAG-29 — Preserve measurement observability in the physical design.** Provide safe, appropriate points and procedures to measure mains modes, magnetic temperature, CT waveforms, tank current/voltage, filter impedance, and unfiltered-versus-filtered results without changing loop geometry enough to invalidate results. Evidence: test-access drawing and measurement repeatability. The book repeatedly distinguishes simulation from constructed-filter measurement, including network-analyzer comparison (printed pp. 77–85, PDF pp. 78–86) and transformer parasitic measurement setups (pp. 123–127, PDF pp. 124–128). **Temper inference:** measurement access is a review criterion because several other scores depend on it; it is not a construction instruction from the book.

## Minimum calculation and test package

1. **Identity and configuration:** pin manifest and full board hash; record enclosure/coil and firmware revisions separately. Make a controlled list of magnetic and filter parts, their exact datasheet revisions, winding pinouts, and supplier status.
2. **Operating envelope:** capture line, bus, bridge, tank, CMC, CT, and rail current/voltage waveforms across hot/cold, pan cases, startup, idle, full power, trip, and defined faults. State measurement uncertainty and probe bandwidth.
3. **Loss and temperature:** calculate DCR and AC copper loss with waveform harmonics; estimate core flux/loss from volt-seconds and material data; measure component hot spots at equilibrium in the relevant enclosure. Separate self-heating from heat conducted/radiated by nearby parts.
4. **Frequency behavior:** measure or model `Zcm`, `Zdm`, `R/X/Z`, CT transfer, coil/pan impedance, filter source/load impedances, and resonances. Compare mounted results to the model; record any fixture and probe effect.
5. **EMI:** establish baseline spectra, mode decomposition, near-field paths, and line/coil-cable/chassis coupling. Test one change at a time, including load and supply transients, before accepting a component or placement change.
6. **Safety and durability:** independently review exact-part insulation, working and transient voltage, creepage/clearance, PE bond, X/Y qualification, leakage/touch current, surge, abnormal temperature, and fault behavior under the applicable current product requirements. The book supplies conceptual design prompts, not certification limits.

## Highest-priority unknowns for Temper
