**How to score when eventually applied.** For each applicable item: **0** = requirement or rating violated under a credible operating or fault case; **1** = serious concern, missing required protective mechanism, or a plausible unclosed margin; **2** = coherent design calculation with candidate part and layout evidence but required bench/fabrication verification pending; **3** = verified margin over documented operating envelope using current datasheets, actual geometry, and suitable measured evidence; **U** = information absent or not yet examined; **NA** = justified inapplicable with named reason. A digital schematic or DRC pass cannot by itself earn 3 on electrothermal, transient, EMC, or fault criteria. Avoid averaging these scores into a release verdict: safety-critical zeros and unknowns must remain visible. The book teaches circuit behavior; product safety limits, isolation geometry, compliance, and component qualification require current datasheets and applicable standards.

The book's device tables, prices, and named parts are examples from roughly a decade ago. Use their *comparison method* but select against current manufacturer data, lifecycle, and distributor availability. The distinctions below are deliberate: **Book** states what the source supports; **Temper use** is an inference or review action for this cooker.

## A. Passive components, wiring, and parts selection

**AOE-01 — Define the real operating envelope before accepting a rating.** **Book:** Ch. 1 §1.6.3–4, p. 32–34 (PDF 62–64) uses capacitor tolerance and load behavior to show why a neat nominal ripple calculation can mislead. Ch. 3 §3.5.2/§3.5.4D, p. 192/199 (PDF 222/229) warns that headline switch current and power assume favorable case temperatures while low on-resistance is specified at a different temperature. **Review question/evidence:** For every stressed part, are voltage, current, frequency, duty, ambient, hot-case, startup, shutdown, and fault envelopes documented against the *same* datasheet conditions? Use a stress table with min/nom/max and margin, not a single nominal BOM value. **Trade-off:** extra derating may enlarge packages and cost; marginal nominal parts invite temperature-dependent failure. **Temper use:** apply first to bridge devices, DC link, resonant parts, shunts, diodes, drivers, and connectors.

**AOE-02 — Size capacitors by effective function, not marked microfarads.** **Book:** Ch. 1 §1.4.5, p. 28 (PDF 58) explicitly lists series resistance, inductance, and dielectric absorption; §1.6.3–4, p. 32–34 (PDF 62–64), notes broad capacitance tolerance in rectifier filters; Ch. 5 §5.6.2, p. 300–301 (PDF 330–331), shows leakage and dielectric memory matter for stored analog values. **Review question/evidence:** At actual DC bias, temperature, AC ripple spectrum, lifetime, and mounting, what capacitance, ESR, ESL, ripple-current/temperature margin, and leakage remain? Record current manufacturer curves, thermal model, and circuit purpose for each capacitor class. **Trade-off:** high-value ceramics save area but can lose effective capacitance and add nonlinear behavior; film or electrolytic alternatives cost volume and have different losses/lifetime. **Temper use:** separate bulk bus energy storage, local commutation, rail bypass, timing, and sensing filters; they do not share one selection rule.

**AOE-03 — Treat inductors and transformers as lossy magnetic parts.** **Book:** Ch. 1 §1.5.1–2, p. 28–31 (PDF 58–61), distinguishes ideal stored energy from winding resistance, core losses, and self-capacitance (illustrated on p. 29, Fig. 1.51; PDF 59). Ch. 9 §9.6.4–5, p. 641–646 (PDF 670–675), adds practical switched-inductor energy transfer. **Review question/evidence:** Does each magnetic maintain inductance and insulation at peak current, frequency, temperature, and asymmetry; are copper/core losses and winding hot spots estimated? Ask for saturation and loss curves, winding resistance, thermal rise, and placement/coupling evidence. **Trade-off:** higher inductance can reduce ripple but increase resistance, capacitance, size, or saturation risk. **Temper use:** apply to power conversion, EMI chokes, sensing transformers, and any resonant network with their distinct stress spectra.

**AOE-04 — Select rectifiers and clamps for transients, not DC forward drop alone.** **Book:** Ch. 1 §1.6.6–8, p. 35–40 (PDF 65–70), explains inductive energy and diode protection; Ch. 3 §3.5.4E, p. 199 (PDF 229), explains MOSFET body-diode reverse recovery and its sharp current interruption. **Review question/evidence:** For each freewheel, bridge, TVS, or clamp path, where does stored energy flow during turn-off and abnormal timing? Need reverse-voltage, surge/repetitive current, recovery charge, temperature, clamp-energy, and resulting overshoot evidence. **Trade-off:** a low-drop part may have leakage/capacitance or recovery behavior unsuited to the node; stronger clamps add loss and may slow transitions. **Temper use:** map complete current loops under every full-bridge commutation and fault state, then use current datasheets and transient measurement.

**AOE-05 — Audit connector systems as components with failure modes.** **Book:** Ch. 1 §1.9.2–3, p. 59–61 (PDF 89–91), calls connectors a common reliability weak point, discusses contact security and the trade-off between edge and two-part connections. **Review question/evidence:** Are mating halves, wire gauge, crimp method, pin current at enclosure temperature, cycle rating, retention, keying, touch access, and strain relief identified? Include actual mating drawing and harness route, not only board footprint. **Trade-off:** compact/unlatched parts ease packaging and assembly but worsen mis-mating, contact heating, or service reliability; overbuilt parts take space. **Temper use:** prioritize mains input, coil/power exits, fan, sensors, and controller interfaces.

**AOE-06 — Keep calibration adjustments from becoming the accuracy strategy.** **Book:** Ch. 1 §1.9.5, p. 63 (PDF 93), warns that trimmers are less stable than good fixed resistors and recommends fixed precision components carrying most of the value; Ch. 5 §5.6.1, p. 300 (PDF 330), relates resistor tolerance to gain and heating. **Review question/evidence:** Where are pots or selectable values used, what drift or contact failure could change protection thresholds, and is calibration captured in production records? Prefer stable ratios and explicit firmware calibration for measured quantities where appropriate. **Trade-off:** trim can rescue yield or individual sensors but adds labor and field drift. **Temper use:** protection thresholds should be robust without a fragile manual setting.

**AOE-07 — Account for resistor behavior beyond initial tolerance.** **Book:** Ch. 5 §5.6, p. 299–300 (PDF 329–330), lists temperature coefficient, load cycling, moisture, voltage coefficient, soldering, and long-term stability; §5.6.1 addresses gain networks. **Review question/evidence:** For shunts, dividers, gate resistors, bleeders, and sense ratios, are hot power, pulse energy, voltage, creepage/package, tempco, and ratio drift calculated? Request part-specific pulse/thermal ratings and layout thermal coupling. **Trade-off:** low resistance reduces burden but shrinks signal; high resistance saves power but raises noise/leakage sensitivity. **Temper use:** assess high-voltage divider and current shunt as different resistor design problems.

## B. Power switches and drivers

**AOE-08 — Select the switch topology and technology against actual voltage, loss, and frequency.** **Book:** Ch. 3 §3.5.5–7, p. 201–208 (PDF 231–238), compares MOSFETs, BJTs, and IGBTs: high-voltage MOSFET on-resistance grows, while IGBTs incur a finite on-state drop and different switching behavior. **Review question/evidence:** Does a loss comparison include conduction, turn-on/turn-off, diode/recovery, drive, temperature, and proposed frequency for current candidates? **Trade-off:** smaller conduction loss may mean larger charge or capacitance and worse switching loss. **Temper use:** do not infer that a 2015 example part or crossover voltage is the right present-day choice; compare modern datasheets under the stage15b envelope.

**AOE-09 — Verify fully enhanced gate drive at the hot and low-supply corners.** **Book:** Ch. 3 §3.5.3, p. 193–194 (PDF 223–224), stresses that threshold voltage is specified at tiny current and is not proof of low on-resistance; §3.1.5, p. 138–139 (PDF 168–169), illustrates large FET parameter spread. **Review question/evidence:** At minimum driver supply, maximum junction temperature, tolerances, and droop, does the switch reach the gate voltage at which its on-resistance or saturation drop is *guaranteed*? Include turn-off bias. **Trade-off:** more gate voltage cuts conduction loss but can breach gate oxide or increase drive energy. **Temper use:** check both high and low bridge positions and startup/brownout.

**AOE-10 — Make gate-drive current a transient calculation.** **Book:** Ch. 3 §3.5.4A–B, p. 197–198 (PDF 227–228), explains nonlinear Ciss/Crss, the Miller plateau, gate charge, and the way slow drive increases overlap loss; Fig. 3.100/3.101 on p. 197 was visually checked. **Review question/evidence:** Given actual gate-charge curves, driver source/sink resistance, external gate resistors, loop inductance, frequency, and hot conditions, what are measured transition times and switch energy? A driver data-sheet peak-current headline is insufficient. **Trade-off:** faster edges reduce overlap loss but raise overshoot and EMI; slowing edges may exceed thermal margin or provoke false switching. **Temper use:** evaluate turn-on and turn-off separately on every bridge device.

**AOE-11 — Include output capacitance and body-diode recovery in commutation.** **Book:** Ch. 3 §3.5.4C/E, p. 198–199 (PDF 228–229), describes Coss energy per cycle and stored charge in a conducting body diode. **Review question/evidence:** Which devices conduct through their diode during dead time, and what are Coss/energy and reverse-recovery currents at bus voltage and temperature? Capture switching-node voltage and current with appropriate probes and bandwidth. **Trade-off:** more dead time avoids shoot-through but can increase diode conduction/recovery; less dead time risks overlap. **Temper use:** a resonant load may change current phase, so test commutation across the full operating range.

**AOE-12 — Prove off-state and gate-oxide protection in abnormal states.** **Book:** Ch. 3 §3.5.4F–H, p. 199–200 (PDF 229–230), warns that VGS limits can be far below VDS, gate ESD damage is permanent, and floating gates need a defined discharge path (for an off-board drive, a gate-source resistor). **Review question/evidence:** For power-up, power-down, MCU reset, cable disconnect, driver fault, negative transients, and Miller coupling, what fixes each gate voltage and prevents unintended turn-on? Request gate-source waveform and protection part ratings. **Trade-off:** a strong pull-down or clamp improves immunity but loads the driver and may affect switching; too-large series gate resistance increases loss. **Temper use:** validate stage15b's actual driver scheme, not a generic pull-down value from the book.

**AOE-13 — Separate repetitive switching SOA from fault and linear SOA.** **Book:** Ch. 3 §3.5.1/§3.5.4D and §3.6.3, p. 191–192, 199, 212–214 (PDF 221–222, 229, 242–244), distinguishes high-current low-VDS switching from lower-current linear operation, where paralleling can destabilize. Ch. 9 §9.4.2 develops safe operating area. **Review question/evidence:** For normal PWM, load upset, desaturation, current limit, and startup, does the transient V-I trajectory fit the part's *current* SOA and pulse-width/temperature limits? **Trade-off:** more devices can share switched current, but parallel linear/fault stress and unequal thermal coupling can undermine that benefit. **Temper use:** never use the old book's blanket claim about MOSFET second-breakdown immunity as a modern part qualification.

**AOE-14 — Close the complete junction-to-ambient thermal path.** **Book:** Ch. 3 §3.5.2/§3.5.4D, p. 192, 199 (PDF 222, 229), challenges unrealistic 25°C case current ratings; Ch. 9 §9.4.1, p. 624–627 (PDF 653–656), expands heatsinking. **Review question/evidence:** Is loss predicted at hot junction, then propagated through package, interface, heatsink/PCB, airflow, enclosure, and nearby heaters under sustained duty? Record thermal impedance, boundary conditions, and measured hot-spot validation. **Trade-off:** lower electrical loss may increase gate charge or package thermal resistance; heatsink and copper area cost volume and can affect isolation. **Temper use:** model cooker ambient and blocked/failed fan cases explicitly.

**AOE-15 — Design fault interruption as part of the power path.** **Book:** Ch. 3 §3.5.7, p. 208 (PDF 238), explains that a high-voltage IGBT can be destroyed rapidly under a short and discusses desaturation shutdown; the exact timing is device-dependent. **Review question/evidence:** What event trips overcurrent, desaturation, overvoltage, overtemperature, and lost gate supply; how quickly does energy stop, and where does inductive energy go? Need fault sequence, device withstand curves, probe captures, and independent hardware behavior when firmware stalls. **Trade-off:** aggressive trips protect silicon but may nuisance-trip with startup/inrush or resonant excursions. **Temper use:** bound the protected envelope with actual full-bridge hardware rather than importing the book's example threshold.

## C. Sensing, analog accuracy, filtering, and return paths

**AOE-16 — Write an error budget for each protected or controlled quantity.** **Book:** Ch. 5 §5.1.2–§5.3, p. 293–296 (PDF 323–326), shows that precision parts alone do not make a precision instrument; errors from bias current times source resistance, offset, temperature, and leakage accumulate. §5.5.1, p. 299 (PDF 329), gives a worked input-referred budget including drift and PCB leakage. **Review question/evidence:** For each current, bus-voltage, temperature, pan, and interlock signal, what is the input-referred worst-case and measured uncertainty at the actual trip/feedback thresholds? Carry component tolerance, offset, drift, ADC reference, noise, common-mode, and timing into a single table. **Trade-off:** tighter parts cost money; a better topology or calibration may recover accuracy more cheaply. **Temper use:** safety thresholds need defensible worst-case bounds; production measurement can qualify less-critical accuracy when a datasheet parameter is loose.

**AOE-17 — Verify precision resistor ratios and shunt temperature, not just absolute resistance.** **Book:** Ch. 5 §5.6–§5.6.1, p. 299–300 (PDF 329–330), explains matched ratios, temperature coefficient, load heating, cycling and moisture. **Review question/evidence:** How much do dividers or differential-amplifier ratios move across resistor self-heating and nearby power-stage heat? For shunts, include four-terminal pickup, pulse/continuous power, thermal EMFs where relevant, and amplifier input impedance. **Trade-off:** lower shunt resistance reduces heat and burden but reduces signal-to-noise; precision networks improve matching but may constrain voltage/power spacing. **Temper use:** trace physical Kelvin points and thermal gradients before treating nominal resistance as measured current.

**AOE-18 — Check analog front-end common-mode range, input return path, and recovery.** **Book:** Ch. 4 §4.2.7/§4.4, p. 231, 243 (PDF 261, 273), says ideal op-amp rules fail at input/output saturation. Ch. 5 §5.16.7–8, p. 366 (PDF 395), warns that floating differential inputs need a DC bias path and an instrumentation amplifier can internally saturate despite legal external common-mode voltage. **Review question/evidence:** At all bus and shunt polarities, startup, overrange, loss of sensor connection, and minimum rails, are input common-mode and output swing legal and is recovery time acceptable? Use amplifier transfer plots and bench overdrive waveforms. **Trade-off:** rail-to-rail and wide common-mode devices simplify supply rails but may bring crossover errors, lower bandwidth, or noise. **Temper use:** an ADC pin seeing a plausible value does not prove its upstream amplifier stayed linear.

**AOE-19 — Preserve differential sensing in the presence of mismatch and switching fields.** **Book:** Ch. 5 §5.16.3–4, p. 365–366 (PDF 394–395), explains that mismatched source impedances erode CMRR and that long sensor leads can turn common-mode RF into apparent DC errors; Fig. 5.85 (PDF 396) shows balanced filtering and clamps. **Review question/evidence:** Are positive and negative sense legs physically paired, with matched input filters, bounded common-mode voltage, adequate input protection, and no asymmetric leakage? Measure at operating bridge frequency and with cable movement. **Trade-off:** more input filtering rejects switching noise but delays fault detection and can interact with input bias/ADC sampling. **Temper use:** prioritise current and temperature sensing near the bridge/coil and any off-board sensor harness.

**AOE-20 — Set bandwidth from control and protection timing, then budget noise.** **Book:** Ch. 6 §6.2.5–6, p. 399–401 (PDF 428–430), shows amplitude rejection versus phase delay, overshoot, ringing, and settling trade-offs. Ch. 8 §8.1/§8.13, p. 473–478, 561–564 (PDF 502–507, 590–593), relates noise to bandwidth and warns that 1/f noise cannot use a flat-density shortcut. **Review question/evidence:** What signal bandwidth is needed for a trip or control loop, what alias bands exist, and what latency/phase does each analog and digital filter add? Show response curves, noise integration, and measured step response. **Trade-off:** narrower filters improve noise immunity while slowing protection and distorting transients. **Temper use:** separate fast hardware protection from slower telemetry/filtering where the required times differ.

**AOE-21 — Route return currents so power drops do not become measurements.** **Book:** Ch. 8 §8.16.2, p. 582–583 (PDF 611–612), Fig. 8.124 (visually checked), contrasts a low-level amplifier with and without shared high-current ground/supply drops; the book cautions that a single ‘ground mecca’ is only a crutch. **Review question/evidence:** For each switching state, where do the bridge, gate driver, auxiliary supply, and sensor return currents flow through copper, planes, and connectors? Overlay return loops on layer images and measure ground shift under load. **Trade-off:** indiscriminate plane splits can force long return detours; thoughtful local current-loop placement and differential sensing preserve continuity. **Temper use:** assess real routed stage15b layers and harness, not schematic net names alone.

**AOE-22 — Test radiated, capacitive, and magnetic coupling as distinct paths.** **Book:** Ch. 8 §8.16.1, p. 579–582 (PDF 608–611), distinguishes inputs/outputs, capacitive coupling, magnetic loops, and cable/trace crosstalk. It recommends separation, shielding or lower impedance for electric-field pickup and small loop area/twisted pairs for magnetic pickup. **Review question/evidence:** Which high-dv/dt copper and high-di/dt loops lie near each sensor or interface; do cable changes, enclosure position, and load alter offsets? Capture near-field maps or targeted oscilloscope spectra and probe position sensitivity. **Trade-off:** shielding can help one coupling mode yet add capacitance or complicate protective-earth paths. **Temper use:** treat coil-field pickup and switch-node electric-field pickup separately.

**AOE-23 — Make decoupling and supply filtering stable and local.** **Book:** Ch. 8 §8.16.2, p. 582–583 (PDF 611–612), notes that extra ceramic capacitors plus rail inductance can create unintended resonance; Fig. 8.125 shows separate low-level supply decoupling. Ch. 9 §9.3.13, p. 619 (PDF 648), treats ripple filtering. **Review question/evidence:** Are local rail current pulses returned close to each driver/ADC, while input filters and regulator compensation stay stable across load and capacitor tolerances? Need impedance or transient measurements over the relevant spectrum. **Trade-off:** low-ESR ceramics reduce high-frequency voltage drop but may remove damping; added RC/ferrite filtering can cause rail droop or oscillation. **Temper use:** gate-drive bypass and quiet analog rails serve different current pulses and should be reviewed separately.

## D. Mains input, auxiliary conversion, and fault energy

**AOE-24 — Follow the complete mains protection and isolation path.** **Book:** Ch. 9 §9.5.1, p. 629–632 (PDF 658–661), covers equipment fusing, protective-earth/chassis connection, line filters, X/Y safety capacitors, surge suppressors, and discharge paths. **Review question/evidence:** Where are line/neutral/PE identified and protected, where are X/Y components used, what are fuse interrupting and fault-current ratings, and what happens when a suppressor ages into partial conduction? Inspect exact certified part markings, schematic, layout, enclosure, harness, and applicable current standards. **Trade-off:** filtering and protection reduce disturbance but introduce leakage, standby loss, service parts, and thermal failure modes. **Temper use:** the book is conceptual guidance; it does not establish cooker compliance, creepage values, or permission to energize stage15b.

**AOE-25 — Size fuse and input parts from actual RMS, inrush, and fault waveforms.** **Book:** Ch. 9 §9.5.1B, p. 630–631 (PDF 659–660), explains that rectifier/capacitor input current has a small conduction angle, so RMS heating and fuse behavior are not inferred from average current; a slow-blow fuse accommodates normal inrush. §9.8.3B, p. 669 (PDF 698), illustrates startup inrush. **Review question/evidence:** Are input fuse I²t, interrupt capacity, normal RMS current, inrush envelope, bridge/trace/connector surge capability, and fault clearing coordinated at voltage and temperature extremes? Record instrumented starts and actual candidate-fuse curves. **Trade-off:** too-fast/low a fuse nuisance-opens; too-large/slow a fuse may leave a board fault energized. **Temper use:** no fixed multiplier from the book should replace a protection-coordination calculation and tests.

**AOE-26 — Account for stored energy and discharge after power removal.** **Book:** Ch. 9 §9.5.1E, p. 631–632 (PDF 660–661), points out that across-line capacitors and high-voltage DC storage can remain charged after unplugging; bleeders trade discharge time for standby heat. **Review question/evidence:** What touchable nodes retain voltage after each disconnect/fuse/open-switch state, and what is the worst-case discharge curve with component tolerance or one failed element? Measure decay at line high and maximum capacitance, and check bleeder voltage/power/pulse reliability. **Trade-off:** a low bleeder resistance improves discharge but consumes standby power and runs hot; an active discharge circuit adds failure modes. **Temper use:** evaluate both line filter and DC-link energy, under the enclosure's actual service access model.

**AOE-27 — Check auxiliary supply behavior during startup, brownout, and power failure.** **Book:** Ch. 9 §9.8.3C, p. 669–670 (PDF 698–699), explains the chicken-and-egg startup of an auxiliary winding and the need for a startup source; §9.3 and §9.6 distinguish regulator dropouts and switcher dynamics. **Review question/evidence:** With line ramps, low line, abrupt loss of line, load transients, or stalled fan, do gate drivers and sensing rails remain valid until outputs are safely off? Capture undervoltage sequence and fault response, not only steady-state voltages. **Trade-off:** larger hold-up improves controlled shutdown but increases capacitor volume/inrush; simpler supplies can rely on independent hardware shutdown if proved. **Temper use:** validate the actual stage15b rail architecture before assigning a topology-specific finding.

**AOE-28 — Use reference designs as empirical starting points and test their parasitic omissions.** **Book:** Ch. 9 §9.8.3–4, p. 668–672 (PDF 697–701), compares ideal converter waveforms with turn-off spikes, ringing from leakage inductance and capacitance, hard-switching loss, snubbers, and a full manufacturer reference design. **Review question/evidence:** Which assumptions of a driver or converter reference design still hold at Temper's board geometry, magnetics, switching frequency, and load? Need actual node waveforms, snubber dissipation, and current-loop identification. **Trade-off:** snubbers/damping suppress spikes and EMI but dissipate energy; soft switching can improve loss but is sensitive to load and timing. **Temper use:** the full-bridge resonant power stage has its own commutation conditions, not the book's flyback example.

## E. Isolation, external interfaces, and conversion

**AOE-29 — Treat an isolator as a timed power-and-signal channel.** **Book:** Ch. 12 §12.7.1–3, p. 843–847 (PDF 872–876), contrasts phototransistor CTR/speed, logic isolators, and isolated gate-driver output stages. The gate-driver section explicitly calls out common-mode transient immunity (CMTI), output-side supply, and desaturation shutdown. **Review question/evidence:** At maximum switch-node slew, temperature, aging, and minimum input drive, is logic delivered promptly, with adequate CMTI and output current? Inspect exact isolator and insulation-system certifications, propagation skew, output UVLO, and fault behavior. **Trade-off:** higher isolation/CMTI and integrated protection may add delay, power, price, or package area. **Temper use:** apply only where stage15b has an actual isolation boundary; do not assume an optocoupler package alone makes the system safe.

**AOE-30 — Prove high-side supply hold-up for every commanded pattern.** **Book:** Ch. 12 §12.7.3, p. 846–847 (PDF 875–876), illustrates a bootstrap supply whose capacitor charges only while the floating output is low; Ch. 3 §3.5.3, p. 194 (PDF 224), discusses high-side drivers and gate-current demand. **Review question/evidence:** If a high-side gate supply is bootstrapped, how long can it stay high at maximum duty and startup before UVLO; can the chosen modulation pattern guarantee refresh? Include leakage, quiescent load, gate charge, diode drop, temperature, and measured waveform. **Trade-off:** bootstrap is simple and compact but constrains sustained duty; isolated or charge-pump supply may remove that limit at the cost of parts and noise. **Temper use:** mark NA if stage15b uses another supply architecture.

**AOE-31 — Protect every off-board logic/sensor input at the physical boundary.** **Book:** Ch. 12 §12.1.5, p. 804–805 (PDF 833–834), explains ESD paths at a connector and shows series impedance, clamps, and Schmitt/RC options; Ch. 5 §5.16.4, p. 365–367 (PDF 394–396), treats overload and RF on sensor lines. **Review question/evidence:** What current flows through input clamps for ESD, miswire, hot-plug, and power-off injection, and where does that current return? Check signal validity after protection, including leakage and input capacitance. **Trade-off:** stronger clamps and filters can degrade high-impedance or fast signals. **Temper use:** current standards and system-level ESD tests, not the book's HBM example alone, set qualification.

**AOE-32 — Route fast digital signals with a defined return and termination when needed.** **Book:** Ch. 12 §12.9.1–§12.10.2, p. 856–861 (PDF 885–890), shows logic ground bounce from output current pulses, local bypass, and reflections on longer wires; Ch. 14 §14.7.1–2, p. 1032–1034 (PDF 1063–1065), stresses part-specific SPI timing and bus behavior. **Review question/evidence:** At the actual edge rate and cable/trace length, are clock, reset, chip-select, and fault lines immune to overshoot, return-path discontinuity, and false edges? Compare source/load impedances, scope traces with short ground methods, and interface timing. **Trade-off:** damping and termination cost current or edge time but can turn fragile logic into deterministic signaling. **Temper use:** give fault-disable and gate timing lines priority over ordinary telemetry.

**AOE-33 — Design ADC channel filtering and sampling together.** **Book:** Ch. 13 §13.5.1, p. 900–902 (PDF 931–933), demonstrates aliasing and the guard-band advantage of oversampling; §13.10.2, p. 940–941 (PDF 971–972), distinguishes instantaneous SAR sampling from integrating ADC averaging. **Review question/evidence:** For each ADC path, are source impedance, acquisition interval, sample capacitor loading, reference stability, sample rate, analog anti-alias filter, and protection latency all compatible? Measure channel step/settling and known injected switching tones. **Trade-off:** oversampling relaxes analog filter sharpness but consumes ADC/CPU bandwidth; averaging reduces noise but adds delay. **Temper use:** derive the required response from the control and fault budget first, then select conversion settings.

**AOE-34 — Select ADC/DAC architecture by the error that matters to the loop.** **Book:** Ch. 13 §13.4–§13.5, p. 899–902 (PDF 930–933), distinguishes INL, DNL/monotonicity, and conversion speed; §13.10.1–3, p. 938–942 (PDF 969–973), compares delta-sigma and SAR latency, noise, power, and reference requirements. **Review question/evidence:** Does the chosen converter or ESP32-S3 peripheral meet effective bits, absolute accuracy, monotonicity, channel simultaneity, latency, and reference/supply behavior at the intended sampling rate? **Trade-off:** high nominal resolution can be meaningless after front-end error and EMI; a faster SAR path may need more filtering or averaging than a slower integrating path. **Temper use:** check actual firmware timing and current MCU datasheet rather than judging from ADC bit count.

## F. Buildability, procurement, and trustworthy measurements

**AOE-35 — Make each BOM line a buildable, traceable choice.** **Book:** Ch. 1 §1.10.1, p. 65–66 (PDF 95–96), and Appendix C §C.1–3, p. 1104–1105 (PDF 1135–1136), show how small SMT packages reduce parasitics and area while complicating hand prototyping, identification, and rework; package markings may be ambiguous or absent. **Review question/evidence:** Does each populated reference resolve to an exact manufacturer part, package, footprint/land pattern, polarity/pin-1, assembly process, inspection method, and allowed alternates with equivalent *electrical and mechanical* limits? Compare current manufacturer package drawings with Gerber pads, stencil, courtyard, and real assembly capability. **Trade-off:** miniature packages can improve switching parasitics and density but reduce manual inspection and repair yield; bigger packages increase loop length and board area. **Temper use:** the book does not teach contemporary DFM acceptance rules, so exact footprint, solder-joint, thermal-via, and AOI/X-ray decisions need fabricator/assembler evidence rather than a package-size preference imported from 2015.

**AOE-36 — Qualify supply and lifecycle without mistaking the book's directory for live inventory.** **Book:** Appendix K, p. 1150–1151 (PDF 1181–1182), advocates distributor searches for parts and warns that availability/price aggregators can be unreliable; Ch. 15 §15.10.3, p. 1094 (PDF 1125), treats ecosystem, tooling, package and power as selection criteria for a controller. **Review question/evidence:** For each single-source or safety-critical item, are current status, authorized supply, lead time, minimum order, alternates, counterfeit control, revision/PCN monitoring, and second-source compatibility recorded with check date? For controller or driver ICs, include toolchain/debug and firmware pin/peripheral constraints. **Trade-off:** a theoretically best component may have fragile supply or require expensive requalification; easy availability may cost efficiency or space. **Temper use:** current lifecycle and stock status are *new evidence to collect*, not a fact this 2015 book can establish. A 2015 price, vendor roster, or example MPN is instructional history only.

**AOE-37 — Make the measurement setup part of the evidence.** **Book:** Appendix O §O.1.6, p. 1161 (PDF 1192), points out that a grounded oscilloscope probe clip is connected to protective earth and can short a live node; §O.2, p. 1163 (PDF 1194), explains how digital scope sample, peak-detect, and averaging modes can hide or expose short events. **Review question/evidence:** For every claimed overshoot, ripple, startup, fault, or thermal margin, are probe isolation/rating, return connection, bandwidth, sample rate, trigger mode, current-probe transfer, and operating corner documented? ## Application order and open inputs

Apply the rubric in this order when the pinned candidate is reviewed: (1) freeze board/provenance, exact BOM, enclosure and mains/harness assumptions; (2) map energy and return paths, including normal commutation and each credible fault; (3) calculate device, capacitor, magnetic, resistor, fuse and connector stresses at minimum/maximum line, load, temperature and control states; (4) review layer geometry, gate/sense loops, isolation and assembly drawings against those calculations; (5) plan controlled bench characterization from low-energy tests through instrumented operating corners and fault tests; (6) close each item with part-specific data and recorded measurements. A safety or protective-function concern should be resolved before tuning efficiency or BOM cost. Nothing in this book substitutes for an applicable safety standard or a qualified mains test plan.

Inputs still needed at application time include the exact stage15b schematic, routed layers, stackup and copper weights; controlled BOM with candidate datasheets and package drawings; line range and surge category; coil/pan and switching-frequency range; expected continuous/peak power, current phase and duty; gate-driver rails, UVLO and fault timing; enclosure thermal boundary and fan-failure behavior; sensor accuracy and protection latency requirements; isolation/PE and access model; manufacturer/assembler limits; and reproducible lab waveforms. If an input is absent, use **U**, not a guessed score.
