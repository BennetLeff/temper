The book's recurring method is to identify a waveform and an allowable disturbance, translate the **complete current loop** into an electrical model, predict the result, and measure to test the model. Chapter 1 §§1.9–1.14 (`ch01.xhtml#ch01lev1sec9` through `#ch01lev1sec14`) supports making calculations early and using measurements for risk reduction. Appendix A (`app01.xhtml`) opens by cautioning against following a rule blindly; Appendix B (`app02.xhtml`) says a rule of thumb is useful for estimating effects, not for final acceptance. This matters here because the book's illustrative 50-ohm traces, 100-ps edges, BGA power planes and 5% rail tolerance do not set limits for an induction cooker.

The transfer has three levels:

| Book concept | Directly useful for Temper | Translation or limit |
|---|---|---|
| Loop current, mutual inductance, return paths | Bridge commutation loops, gate-drive loops, current-sense and control return paths | The book treats mostly low-voltage digital loops; a resonant power stage also needs device-stress, thermal, insulation and fault analysis from other sources. |
| Edge bandwidth and impedance discontinuities | Fast bridge/gate edges, digital control and off-board I/O | Use measured **rise/fall time and ringing**, not only switching frequency. Apply line termination only where flight time and receiver noise margin justify it. |
| PDN impedance and mounted capacitor ESL | Low-voltage logic rails, gate-drive supplies, local bypass | Distinguish these from DC-link bulk energy storage and resonant tank capacitors, which have different current, voltage, heating and fault duties. |
| Coupling and differential-mode conversion | Sensitive current/temperature sensing, isolated interfaces, cables | Quantify the actual aggressor spectrum and victim susceptibility. A differential pair still has return-current and common-mode behavior. |
| S-parameters, TDR and GHz loss | Connectors, cables or fast interfaces if present | May be unnecessary for short, slow control nets. Conventional switching-node probing, near-field scans and transient simulation may be more useful for the power stage. |

## Shared assessment scale

Use one result per criterion: **0** = observed failure against a stated requirement; **1** = serious concern, with an identified mechanism or missing margin; **2** = analysis supports the design but the relevant worst case lacks independent verification; **3** = requirement and margin are verified by appropriate analysis and measurement; **U** = unknown because essential evidence or a requirement is absent; **NA** = justified inapplicability. Never average these into a readiness score. Record the requirement, test condition, predicted value, observed value, margin, evidence location, candidate hash and reviewer. A criterion with no defined noise limit or no waveform belongs at **U**, even if the artwork looks tidy. This scale is our review convention, not Bogatin's.

## Rubric

Each item states the question, evidence for a later review, and the principal design trade-off. “Source” is a verified EPUB subsection; “Temper inference” is the proposed application and requires checking on the pinned design.

### A. Define the electrical problem before judging geometry

**SI-01 — Edge and disturbance inventory.** For each bridge control, gate drive, clock, sensor, communication and off-board net, what are the worst-case voltage/current slew rates, pulse widths and relevant spectral bands? Require source-device models or representative waveforms, operating states and an explicit high-frequency cutoff for analysis. Temper inference: the resonant operating frequency is not a substitute for bridge `dv/dt` or gate/control rise time. Trade-off: slowing a gate edge may reduce coupled noise but can increase switching loss and device heating; faster edges can reverse that balance. Source: ch02 §§2.9–2.16 (`ch02.xhtml#ch02lev1sec9` through `#ch02lev1sec16`), especially §2.10's bandwidth/rise-time approximation and §2.13's distinction between clock frequency and edge bandwidth. Do not use `0.35/tr` as an exact spectrum for arbitrary power waveforms.

**SI-02 — Explicit noise and timing budgets.** Has each susceptible pin or rail a limit in volts, timing, or functional error rate, across tolerances and operating modes? Require device thresholds, timing windows, common-mode range, rail tolerance, and fault thresholds from exact datasheets and system requirements. Temper inference: a power-stage noise budget must cover unwanted turn-on, sense error and controller reset, not just digital bit errors. Trade-off: stricter budgets demand routing, filtering, shielding or component cost; a budget derived from actual safety and control margins avoids needless additions. Source: ch01 §§1.1–1.6 (`ch01.xhtml#ch01lev1sec1` to `#ch01lev1sec6`), ch13 §§13.2–13.5 (`ch13.xhtml#ch13lev1sec2` to `#ch13lev1sec5`).

**SI-03 — Model fidelity and domain.** Are the models accurate over the event's time/frequency range and operating bias? Require a model list for drivers, devices, parasitics, capacitors, stackup and probes, with known validation ranges. Temper inference: a beautiful frequency plot is weak evidence if the capacitor ESR/ESL, switch capacitances or layout loop geometry are generic. Trade-off: a simple lumped model is useful early and easier to debug; use field or measured models for geometries where lumped assumptions fail. Source: ch01 §§1.10–1.14 (`ch01.xhtml#ch01lev1sec10` through `#ch01lev1sec14`), ch02 §2.15 (`ch02.xhtml#ch02lev1sec15`), ch03 §§3.8–3.10 (`ch03.xhtml#ch03lev1sec8` to `#ch03lev1sec10`).

**SI-04 — Prediction and measurement agreement.** Did measured waveforms test a predeclared mechanism and bound the prediction? Require schematic/model revision, probe geometry and bandwidth, calibration, raw traces, and explanation of discrepancy. Temper inference: simulation should locate risks before a build; measurements should identify the model's errors and update it. Trade-off: instrument bandwidth and probe loop can manufacture or hide ringing. Source: ch01 §§1.13–1.14 (`ch01.xhtml#ch01lev1sec13`, `#ch01lev1sec14`), ch02 §2.14 (`ch02.xhtml#ch02lev1sec14`).

### B. Current loops, return paths and switching noise

**SI-05 — Enumerate complete fast-current loops.** Can the outbound and return current paths be drawn for every large `di/dt` event, including input bypass, each bridge commutation state, gate-driver charging/discharging, snubber, low-voltage supply and sense loop? Require annotated top/bottom copper and a state-by-state current-loop drawing with actual component pads and vias. Temper inference: the most important return conductor can differ between commutation states. Trade-off: shorter and closer forward/return paths reduce loop inductance, but may concentrate heat or collide with creepage/assembly constraints. Source: ch06 §§6.7–6.9 (`ch06.xhtml#ch06lev1sec7` to `#ch06lev1sec9`), ch07 §7.13 (`ch07.xhtml#ch07lev1sec13`).

**SI-06 — Bound loop inductance and voltage overshoot.** Has each critical loop's effective inductance been estimated or measured, with `L di/dt` compared to device, driver and sense margins? Require loop geometry, parasitic extraction or calibrated transient model, and waveform under intended worst-case switching. Temper inference: resistance-only width checks cannot establish transient voltage margin. Trade-off: copper width helps DC loss and heating, while return proximity and loop area often control inductive overshoot; adding distant parallel copper can yield less benefit than expected. Source: ch06 §§6.7–6.8 (`ch06.xhtml#ch06lev1sec7`, `#ch06lev1sec8`), ch13 §§13.13–13.15 (`ch13.xhtml#ch13lev1sec13` to `#ch13lev1sec15`).

**SI-07 — Shared-return contamination.** Where do switch, gate, auxiliary supply and measurement currents share copper, vias, package pins or connector contacts? Require return-path overlay and voltage-drop estimate for the shared impedance; then capture relevant pin-to-pin voltage during switching. Temper inference: the right question is voltage between the circuit's actual reference points, not whether a net is named “ground.” Trade-off: one broad plane can provide a low-inductance return, while careless shared entry points or slots inject noise into sensing. Fragmenting a plane to “isolate” circuits may create a worse detour. Source: ch06 §6.7 (`ch06.xhtml#ch06lev1sec7`), ch07 §§7.1, 7.13–7.14 (`ch07.xhtml#ch07lev1sec1`, `#ch07lev1sec13`, `#ch07lev1sec14`).

**SI-08 — Reference continuity across transitions.** Does every fast single-ended net retain a nearby return path through layer changes, connectors, cutouts and domain crossings? Require layer-by-layer overlays, transition maps, stitching or deliberate return coupling, and impedance/noise estimate. Temper inference: a via through a plane field or an isolation slot is a different electrical route from its visible signal trace. Trade-off: adding a return stitch may improve signal integrity but must respect isolation and creepage; where isolation prevents it, use an appropriate isolated interface or controlled alternative. Source: ch07 §7.14 (`ch07.xhtml#ch07lev1sec14`), ch11 §11.18 (`ch11.xhtml#ch11lev1sec18`). The book's example of a tightly coupled differential pair crossing a return gap is conditional and does **not** override insulation requirements.

**SI-09 — Current path proximity and parallel conductors.** Where are opposite-current paths close and where do same-direction parallels interact? Require annotated geometry and a loop-inductance comparison for meaningful alternatives, including via and plane contact effects. Temper inference: “more vias” can lower resistance yet provide less inductance improvement if closely coupled in the wrong way or if plane spreading dominates. Trade-off: via count costs board space and manufacturability; optimize the measured total path. Source: ch06 §6.7 and its Figure 6-11 (`ch06.xhtml#ch06lev1sec7`), ch13 §§13.13–13.15 (`ch13.xhtml#ch13lev1sec13` to `#ch13lev1sec15`).

### C. Local supplies and capacitors

**SI-10 — Separate capacitor jobs.** For each capacitor, is its purpose identified as local IC bypass, gate-drive pulse supply, regulator/bulk energy, DC link, resonant tank, snubber or EMI filter? Require exact component duty and its current, voltage, ripple, ESR, ESL, temperature, lifetime and safety class. Temper inference: Chapter 13's low-voltage PDN target-impedance method can guide IC and gate-driver rails; it does not qualify a resonant capacitor or establish DC-link energy and ripple life. Trade-off: consolidating functions may save parts but can push the wrong current through a package or add unwanted coupling. Source: ch13 §§13.1–13.5, 13.12–13.13 (`ch13.xhtml#ch13lev1sec1` to `#ch13lev1sec5`, `#ch13lev1sec12`, `#ch13lev1sec13`).

**SI-11 — Rail impedance versus load spectrum.** For each low-voltage rail, is the impedance seen **at the load pins** low enough over its actual transient-current spectrum? Require load-step or current spectrum, allowable droop, regulator response, PCB/package/capacitor model and rail-voltage capture at the device. Temper inference: evaluate high-side and low-side gate-drive supplies, controller and sense rails separately. Trade-off: lower impedance everywhere may consume space and money; target the spectral bands and rail tolerances that matter. Source: ch13 §§13.2–13.8 (`ch13.xhtml#ch13lev1sec2` to `#ch13lev1sec8`). The book's `Ztarget ≈ allowed droop / transient current` is a starting bound under stated assumptions, not a universal value.

**SI-12 — Mounted, not catalog, bypass inductance.** Is each critical bypass path assessed from active pins through pads, surface traces, vias, plane spreading and return? Require actual placement/stackup and a calculated, extracted or measured loop impedance. Temper inference: a “low-ESL” catalog part can be defeated by long narrow access traces or a distant plane pair. Trade-off: moving a capacitor nearer the switch or IC may worsen heat exposure, assembly or isolation; compare total mounted loop performance. Source: ch13 §§13.13–13.15 (`ch13.xhtml#ch13lev1sec13` to `#ch13lev1sec15`), especially original Figures 13-35 and 13-36. The figure's BGA numbers are examples only.

**SI-13 — Antiresonance and damping.** Do mixed capacitor values, regulator impedance, planes and package parasitics create an impedance peak where the load has energy? Require a rail-by-rail impedance sweep or validated simulation with real ESR/ESL/mounting, and voltage response to representative load steps. Temper inference: the peak matters more than the lowest impedance dip. Trade-off: extra small values can improve one band while creating a higher parallel resonance elsewhere; controlled ESR or damping may help, at the expense of loss. Source: ch13 §§13.16–13.18, 13.22 (`ch13.xhtml#ch13lev1sec16` through `#ch13lev1sec18`, `#ch13lev1sec22`), original Figure 13-44. The chapter explicitly says the optimal value depends on C, ESR and ESL, so “one each per decade” is not a blanket rule.

**SI-14 — Bulk versus fast local energy.** Are the regulator and bulk capacitors assigned the frequencies they can actually support, and are fast switching demands supplied by local low-inductance paths? Require frequency-domain or transient model including regulator control bandwidth and capacitor impedance; demonstrate worst-case start, shutdown and repeated switching. Temper inference: a large remote DC-link capacitor may have ample stored energy and still allow large local overshoot due to access inductance. Trade-off: local film/ceramic additions change stress distribution and resonance; voltage and ripple ratings must be checked. Source: ch13 §§13.6–13.8 and 13.11–13.13 (`ch13.xhtml#ch13lev1sec6` to `#ch13lev1sec8`, `#ch13lev1sec11` to `#ch13lev1sec13`).

**SI-15 — Placement sensitivity and stackup.** Has capacitor location been judged against plane pair depth, separation, via paths and current distribution instead of Euclidean distance alone? Require stackup, mounting-inductance and spreading-inductance comparison for alternatives. Temper inference: “closest to the IC” is a useful prompt but not proof of lowest loop impedance when return access differs. Trade-off: a thinner adjacent power/return cavity improves PDN behavior but affects cost, layer allocation and high-voltage clearance. Source: ch13 §§13.15, 13.23–13.25 (`ch13.xhtml#ch13lev1sec15`, `#ch13lev1sec23` to `#ch13lev1sec25`), original Figures 13-35/36.

### D. Signal routing, discontinuity and timing

**SI-16 — Decide when a net is electrically long.** For each fast control or external signal, compare one-way flight time with its **actual** edge time and noise margin. Require length, stackup velocity estimate, source/load impedance and waveform. Temper inference: the book's rough `TD > 0.2 × rise time` prompts an analysis, not an automatic failure or fixed inch limit. Trade-off: series termination can suppress ringing but adds delay, source dissipation and a source-end shelf. Source: ch07 §7.11 (`ch07.xhtml#ch07lev1sec11`), ch08 §§8.9–8.10 (`ch08.xhtml#ch08lev1sec9`, `#ch08lev1sec10`).

**SI-17 — Termination matched to topology.** If reflections threaten a receiver, does the chosen termination work for all loads and observation points? Require topology, driver impedance, receiver thresholds, simulation/capture at source and receivers. Temper inference: point-to-point source series termination may suit a fast digital line, but a nearby secondary receiver can see the initial half-amplitude shelf. Trade-off: parallel termination draws static current; source termination avoids that but imposes topology limits. Source: ch08 §§8.3–8.5, 8.9–8.10 (`ch08.xhtml#ch08lev1sec3` through `#ch08lev1sec5`, `#ch08lev1sec9`, `#ch08lev1sec10`).

**SI-18 — Discontinuity inventory.** Are neckdowns, branches, stubs, via transitions, test pads, connectors and loads assessed by electrical delay and impedance step? Require net-specific feature map and simulated or TDR-observed effect where risk warrants. Temper inference: tiny artwork details need attention only if the edge is fast enough and noise margin small enough; a long stub or uncontrolled connector may dominate. Trade-off: removing test pads can improve SI but reduce debug access; use alternate probing or design the pad into the model. Source: ch08 §§8.8, 8.11–8.18 (`ch08.xhtml#ch08lev1sec8`, `#ch08lev1sec11` to `#ch08lev1sec18`).

**SI-19 — Delay and matching tied to a timing budget.** Is any length matching derived from setup/hold, skew or phase requirements? Require timing budget including driver, receiver, component, via and trace delays and tolerances. Temper inference: matching all traces wastes routing space; asymmetric gate-drive propagation may matter, but its allowed difference must come from switching/dead-time analysis, not a generic “same length” rule. Trade-off: serpentine compensation can add coupling or discontinuities while solving a delay that never mattered. Source: ch02 §2.13 (`ch02.xhtml#ch02lev1sec13`), ch11 §11.15 (`ch11.xhtml#ch11lev1sec15`).

**SI-20 — Material and trace loss where it matters.** For a high-speed cable or long digital channel, does conductor/dielectric loss degrade edge or eye margin? Require laminate data at relevant frequency, channel length, loss model or measured insertion loss. Temper inference: this may be **NA** for short control nets, whereas DC and AC copper heating must still be considered by a power-layout review. Trade-off: lower-loss laminate or wider traces cost money/space and often do little for short low-speed routes. Source: ch09 §§9.2–9.16 (`ch09.xhtml#ch09lev1sec2` through `#ch09lev1sec16`).

### E. Coupling, sensing and off-board common mode

**SI-21 — Rank aggressor/victim pairs.** Which switching paths and fast clocks run near current sense, zero-cross, temperature, fault and communication nets, and for how long? Require 3D adjacency map, mutual C/L estimate or targeted simulation, victim impedance/noise budget and worst-case simultaneous switching. Temper inference: prioritize paths with high `dv/dt` or `di/dt` and susceptible circuits; parallel run length and return geometry can matter more than minimum spacing at one point. Trade-off: increased spacing is usually robust but competes with board area and insulation corridors. Source: ch10 §§10.2–10.4, 10.8–10.14 (`ch10.xhtml#ch10lev1sec2` to `#ch10lev1sec4`, `#ch10lev1sec8` to `#ch10lev1sec14`).

**SI-22 — Guard trace claims.** If a guard trace is used, has it been compared with the same spacing without the guard, and is its return connection effective over the aggressor spectrum? Require geometry, termination/stitching, simulation or A/B measurement. Temper inference: a floating guard can become a coupled conductor; its apparent benefit may come mainly from the extra spacing required to fit it. Trade-off: guard copper consumes a clearance corridor and can increase parasitic capacitance. Source: ch10 §10.15 (`ch10.xhtml#ch10lev1sec15`), original Figure 10-43. The book's 5-mil example is illustrative, not a spacing design rule for Temper.

**SI-23 — Differential pair return and symmetry.** For any differential sensor or communication interface, are both conductors, their reference, launch, load and connector treated symmetrically? Require interface spec, pair geometry, return-path map, common-mode range and asymmetric-pad/via inventory. Temper inference: “differential” does not mean return current vanishes from the reference plane; it means the modes and symmetry must be managed. Trade-off: tighter coupling can improve compactness and tolerance of poor return conditions, while wider loosely coupled traces can lower resistive loss; choose based on the interface's actual requirement. Source: ch11 §§11.6, 11.15, 11.18–11.19 (`ch11.xhtml#ch11lev1sec6`, `#ch11lev1sec15`, `#ch11lev1sec18`, `#ch11lev1sec19`), original Figure 11-16.

**SI-24 — Common-mode conversion and cables.** Do skew, unbalanced loading, reference gaps or cable exits convert differential or internal switching energy into common-mode current? Require cable/connector pinout, chassis and shield return paths, current-probe measurement or simulation across relevant operating states. Temper inference: a clean differential waveform at the receiver does not prove low cable emissions. Trade-off: filtering/shielding can reduce emission but can introduce leakage, capacitance, isolation or signal-margin consequences. Source: ch11 §§11.15–11.16 (`ch11.xhtml#ch11lev1sec15`, `#ch11lev1sec16`); ch12 §12.15 (`ch12.xhtml#ch12lev1sec15`).

**SI-25 — Crosstalk evidence at the victim.** Are quiet-net errors assessed at the receiving pins under worst-case aggressor states? Require simultaneous scope captures with proper probing, victim threshold/capture timing and a reproducible operating sequence; simulation can rank candidates. Temper inference: generic trace-spacing ratios alone cannot prove a high-voltage switch does not trip a fault input or perturb a current sensor. Trade-off: stronger filtering suppresses spikes but adds delay and can hide real faults; define permitted latency. Source: ch10 §§10.14, 10.17–10.19 (`ch10.xhtml#ch10lev1sec14`, `#ch10lev1sec17` to `#ch10lev1sec19`).

### F. Measurement choice and sign-off evidence

**SI-26 — Instrument matches the question.** Has each risk been assigned a suitable method: rail impedance/load-step, switch/gate transient, TDR for electrically long paths, VNA/S-parameters for channels or decoupling networks, near-field or cable current for emissions? Require stated quantity, port/reference definition, bandwidth, fixture de-embedding and uncertainty. Temper inference: a 50-ohm S-parameter plot is not automatically the operating impedance of a nonlinear bridge; it can characterize an appropriate linear passive subnetwork under stated conditions. Trade-off: sophisticated measurements add fixtures and interpretation risk; use the simplest instrument that directly tests the limit. Source: ch01 §§1.13–1.14 (`ch01.xhtml#ch01lev1sec13` to `#ch01lev1sec14`), ch12 §§12.2–12.7, 12.17 (`ch12.xhtml#ch12lev1sec2` to `#ch12lev1sec7`, `#ch12lev1sec17`).

**SI-27 — Verify uncertainty and worst case.** Have layout, component tolerances, voltage, temperature, operation, source impedance and probe loading been varied enough to establish margin? Require corner matrix and actual versus predicted limits, with the candidate identity attached. Temper inference: a single nominal bench trace is useful diagnosis but does not close a criterion for the full cooker. Trade-off: exhaustive corners are expensive; use first-order sensitivity analysis to prioritize the combinations capable of overturning the result. Source: ch01 §§1.9–1.14 (`ch01.xhtml#ch01lev1sec9` through `#ch01lev1sec14`), ch13 §§13.4–13.5 (`ch13.xhtml#ch13lev1sec4`, `#ch13lev1sec5`).

### G. Basic material parameters that feed the models

**SI-28 — Copper resistance with the right current distribution.** Are DC and frequency-dependent losses estimated from actual copper thickness, width, length and operating spectrum, with temperature effects covered by component/board analysis? Require fabricated geometry, current waveforms, and thermal cross-check. Temper inference: wide copper helps conduction loss, but skin/current crowding and return geometry can alter AC loss and loop inductance. Trade-off: adding copper costs area and can increase capacitive coupling to noisy nodes; heat extraction and insulation constraints may determine its shape. Source: ch04 §§4.2–4.5 (`ch04.xhtml#ch04lev1sec2` to `#ch04lev1sec5`), ch06 §6.16 (`ch06.xhtml#ch06lev1sec16`), ch09 §§9.2–9.4 (`ch09.xhtml#ch09lev1sec2` to `#ch09lev1sec4`). Bogatin's transmission-line treatment informs the model; allowable temperature and ampacity need separate evidence.

**SI-29 — Parasitic capacitance and displacement-current route.** Have high-`dv/dt` nodes' capacitances to nearby control copper, return planes, heatsink/chassis and cables been identified, and does the resulting displacement current have a controlled return? Require physical geometry, capacitance estimate or extraction and measured common-mode/sense effect under switching. Temper inference: extra plane overlap may reduce one inductive path while increasing capacitive injection from a switching node. Trade-off: spacing and selective copper removal reduce capacitance but can lengthen return paths or worsen thermal spreading; evaluate both current mechanisms. Source: ch05 §§5.1, 5.3–5.8 (`ch05.xhtml#ch05lev1sec1`, `#ch05lev1sec3` to `#ch05lev1sec8`), ch07 §7.13 (`ch07.xhtml#ch07lev1sec13`), ch10 §10.2 (`ch10.xhtml#ch10lev1sec2`). Capacitance is not an insulation or creepage acceptance criterion.

### Criterion-specific score anchors

Apply the shared **U/NA** rules first. In this table, **0** requires a demonstrated violation of an applicable requirement or limit; a layout convention alone cannot produce 0. **1** requires an identified physical mechanism and credible risk to margin; merely missing an input is U. **2** requires a quantitative, traceable analysis under stated conditions. **3** requires that analysis plus relevant independent measurement with documented uncertainty and worst-case margin. For process-oriented rows, a violated performance requirement makes 0; an incomplete or unvalidated process on its own is U or 1 according to whether a specific risk is identified.

| ID | 0 — demonstrated failure | 1 — identified concern | 2 — supported prediction | 3 — verified margin |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| SI-01 | Measured edge spectrum exceeds the bandwidth of a specified path/model and breaches its noise or timing limit. | Known fast edge is omitted from the analysis and threatens a named limit. | Edge data and bandwidth cover stated operating corners. | Worst-case edge captures and bounded spectral model confirm margin. |
| SI-02 | Pin, rail or timing limit is exceeded in a specified mode. | Quantified noise or timing estimate approaches a named limit without demonstrated margin. | Budgets and corner calculations predict margin at every named victim. | Pin-level tests across stated corners confirm each budget's margin. |
| SI-03 | Model error demonstrably causes a missed applicable limit violation. | Specific missing parasitic or bias dependence could reverse a predicted margin. | Models cover relevant spectrum, bias and geometry with sensitivity bounds. | Independent measurements bound model error below the available margin. |
| SI-04 | Qualified capture demonstrates a specified functional/noise limit breach. | Prediction and capture materially disagree in a way that threatens margin. | Measurement plan, probe model and predicted waveforms are traceable. | Calibrated captures match bounded predictions and confirm margin. |
| SI-05 | A measured commutation or gate-loop effect breaches a device or noise limit. | Identified omitted return or large loop plausibly creates dangerous `L di/dt`. | All operating-state loops mapped; calculated loop effects fit limits. | Loop-sensitive transients measured across states with margin. |
| SI-06 | Captured overshoot or induced voltage exceeds the applicable rating/limit. | Estimated `L di/dt` approaches a limit or model uncertainty dominates margin. | Extracted/estimated inductance predicts safe worst-case transient. | Calibrated worst-case transient confirms rated voltage/noise margin. |
| SI-07 | Shared-return voltage at a victim exceeds its specified tolerance. | Shared segment and `di/dt` yield a plausible sense or driver error. | Shared impedance and victim voltage are calculated below tolerance. | Differential pin-to-pin capture confirms margin during switching. |
| SI-08 | Return discontinuity causes measured signal/noise limit violation. | A mapped slot/transition has a credible high-frequency detour. | Return transition and its modeled disturbance remain within budget. | Transition-sensitive waveform or impedance measurement confirms margin. |
| SI-09 | Measured path behavior violates the stated transient/rail limit. | Via/parallel layout has a quantified inductance concern. | Total-path comparison predicts adequate inductance and current sharing. | Transient/current-sharing evidence confirms the selected geometry's margin. |
| SI-10 | Exact capacitor exceeds rated ripple, voltage, temperature or life duty. | A named capacitor has overlapping duties with plausible rating conflict. | Per-function stress calculation stays within exact part ratings. | Electrical/thermal stress evidence confirms margin in intended modes. |
| SI-11 | Rail at load pin exceeds allowed droop or noise. | Modeled impedance peak overlaps known load-current energy near the budget. | Rail impedance and load spectrum predict bounded pin droop. | Pin-level load-step or operating captures confirm rail margin. |
| SI-12 | Mounted bypass path yields observed rail or driver limit breach. | Quantified mounting loop inductance undermines nominal capacitance benefit. | Mounted path model predicts sufficient high-frequency support. | Rail/impedance measurement validates mounted model and margin. |
| SI-13 | Antiresonant rail peak causes measured droop above limit. | Modeled peak overlaps plausible load content with inadequate margin. | ESR/ESL/placement model keeps maximum peak below target. | Impedance sweep and transient capture confirm no harmful peak. |
| SI-14 | Local transient exceeds rail/device limit despite bulk capacitance. | Remote bulk access inductance or regulator bandwidth creates a credible gap. | Multiband supply model predicts local voltage margin. | Start, shutdown and repeated-switching captures confirm margin. |
| SI-15 | Selected placement yields measured rail violation under specified load. | Actual stackup/spreading path is materially worse than claimed proximity implies. | Compared mounting and spreading models favor a sufficient layout. | Measured impedance/transient result verifies the selected path. |
| SI-16 | Receiver ringing violates a threshold or timing limit. | Flight time is material against the measured edge and reflections threaten margin. | Source/line/load model predicts acceptable receiver waveform. | Receiver capture at fastest edge confirms noise/timing margin. |
| SI-17 | Termination causes measured threshold, timing or power limit breach. | Topology predicts a source shelf, reflection or dissipation risk. | Multi-observation-point simulation satisfies all budgets. | Source and receiver captures plus power check confirm margin. |
| SI-18 | Specific discontinuity causes measured waveform limit breach. | Mapped stub/branch/via produces a modeled reflection near tolerance. | Net model includes discontinuities and predicts margin. | TDR or receiver waveform validates material discontinuities. |
| SI-19 | Measured path skew violates an interface or switching timing requirement. | Calculated skew is close to a named setup/hold/dead-time budget. | Full timing budget predicts margin without arbitrary matching. | Corner timing captures confirm allowed skew/phase margin. |
| SI-20 | Channel loss violates specified eye, timing or amplitude limit. | Extracted loss is significant against a named channel budget. | Material and trace-loss model predicts channel margin. | Measured channel loss or eye confirms margin at target rate. |
| SI-21 | Aggressor operation causes victim limit breach. | Mapped parallel run and coupling estimate threaten victim budget. | Aggressor/victim model predicts noise below threshold. | Worst-case simultaneous-switching capture confirms victim margin. |
| SI-22 | Guard configuration increases coupled noise enough to breach limit. | Floating/poorly returned guard has a modeled coupling risk. | Guard-versus-spacing comparison predicts adequate margin. | A/B victim measurement confirms benefit and required margin. |
| SI-23 | Pair asymmetry or return disruption violates interface limit. | Specific imbalance threatens differential or common-mode budget. | Pair, return and loading model predicts both modes within limits. | Differential and common-mode measurements confirm margins. |
| SI-24 | Cable common-mode current or emission exceeds an applicable limit. | Defined asymmetry/cable path predicts excessive common-mode drive. | Mode-conversion and cable model predicts compliance margin. | Cable-current or emissions measurement confirms margin. |
| SI-25 | Victim pin crosses its fault/noise limit during aggressor switching. | Coupled transient approaches threshold during a sensitive sample. | Victim waveform and filter-delay model predict both noise and latency margin. | Simultaneous pin captures show noise margin and timely fault response. |
| SI-26 | Qualified measurement contradicts a claimed compliant limit. | Fixture, port or bandwidth error could reverse a specific claimed margin. | Method/fixture uncertainty is bounded within predicted margin. | Calibrated, repeatable instrument result confirms the actual limit. |
| SI-27 | A required operating corner demonstrably violates a limit. | Sensitivity estimate identifies a credible untested margin reversal. | Corner model bounds all declared worst cases with margin. | Selected worst-case measurements validate sensitivity and margin. |
| SI-28 | Copper loss/temperature or voltage drop exceeds applicable limit. | Calculated crowding or temperature rise threatens stated margin. | Geometry/current/thermal model predicts loss and temperature margin. | Current and temperature measurements confirm margin at corners. |
| SI-29 | Displacement current produces measured sense, rail or common-mode limit breach. | Extracted node-to-victim/chassis capacitance implies troublesome injection. | Capacitance/current/return model predicts bounded victim disturbance. | Switching and common-mode captures confirm margin and return route. |

## Decision rules and order of work

| If the evidence shows... | First analysis or action | Caveat |
|---|---|---|
| Large commutation or gate-loop `L di/dt` | Map the complete loop, estimate/extract inductance, compare measured overshoot with device margin | Do not judge only by copper area or trace width. |
| A low-voltage rail droops at a device pin | Measure current step and rail at the pins; model regulator, mounted bypass and antiresonance | Larger nominal C alone may miss an ESL or impedance-peak problem. |
| Fast control-net ringing | Compare line delay to measured edge, map source/load/stubs, test termination in simulation and at the receiver | Termination can create delay or source-end shelves. |
| Sense corruption near a switching node | Identify coupling and shared-return paths; measure victim at its receiver under worst switching | Filtering trades noise rejection for latency and fault detectability. |
| Cable emission or unexpected common-mode current | Inspect asymmetry, shield/chassis route and plane transitions; measure cable current | Receiver signal quality can be good while common-mode emission is bad. |
| An asserted “matched” or “50-ohm” route | Ask what endpoint and standard require that impedance; verify stackup and transitions | Controlled impedance is a conditional system requirement, not a generic PCB virtue. |

**Priority for applying this later:** (1) preserve the candidate hash and derive state-by-state high-energy/current-loop maps; (2) collect exact switching edge, gate-drive and victim-limit data; (3) model critical loop inductance, shared return and supply impedance; (4) identify coupling and off-board pathways; (5) choose focused bench tests and record uncertainty; (6) only then score each item. The book strongly supports this order through chapters 1, 6–8, 10–13, but the specific order is a Temper inference.

**Inputs needed for a real review.** The hash-pinned board and schematic; layer stackup with copper/dielectric thickness and material data; exact BOM and device datasheets; power-stage operating states and resonant waveform; switch/gate voltage and current edge measurements or validated models; gate-driver rail and controller/sense rail loads, tolerances and allowed droop; device absolute/transient ratings; noise/timing/false-trip limits; cable and enclosure/chassis paths; expected temperature corners; and safe test setup with raw captures. For this book's specific SI/PI claims, record which are modeled, which are measured, and which remain hypotheses. EMC compliance, insulation/creepage, thermal reliability and BOM qualification require separate specialist criteria.
