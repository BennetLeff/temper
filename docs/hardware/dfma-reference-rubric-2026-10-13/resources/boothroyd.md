## Strongest source mechanisms

1. **Optimize the assembled system, then validate each manufacturing process.** DFM concerns making each part; DFA concerns joining parts. A locally easier-to-make item can increase joined-system cost: the book explicitly uses a split sheet-metal example where seemingly simpler parts lose after storage, handling, quality, paperwork and assembly are counted. Its redesign cases add material, tooling and assembly before declaring a saving (printed pp. 1, 6, 12–15/PDF 40, 45, 51–54). The three theoretical questions for a distinct part are: required relative motion, necessary different material/isolation, or separation needed to assemble/disassemble other required parts. They are *ideation prompts*, not authorization to delete safety insulation, protective earth, fault containment or service fasteners (pp. 10–11, 82/PDF 49–50, 121).
2. **Count operations and handling penalties, not just item count.** Parts that tangle, nest, flex, slip, or need special orientation and tools cause acquisition costs; poorly located parts require holding while another action is performed. Single-direction build, self-location before release, tool approach, and avoiding flips/adjustments simplify the route (pp. 74–79, 105–112/PDF 113–118, 144–151). Theoretical part count and assembly efficiency are comparison tools; the book itself says final cost needs both part-making and assembly estimates (p. 82/PDF 121). Its printed time standards are not valid Temper labor standards without trials.
3. **Harnesses deserve their own assembly flow.** Wire preparation, cut/strip/terminate, lay-in, secure, route, connect, and test are distinct operations. Connector type, termination method, access, length, breakouts, and testability can dominate mechanical assembly time (pp. 133–144/PDF 172–183; section 4.6 around pp. 163–171/PDF 202–210). Combining boards may eliminate wires in one example, but the book does not discuss Temper isolation, EMI, heat, or service constraints; moving a circuit onto one board cannot be judged from wire count alone.
4. **PCB cost spans bare board and PCBA.** The PCB chapter makes board layer count, thickness, copper, conductor width/spacing, panel utilization, holes, mask/legend, inspection, test, component format, side count, insertion method, setup, programming, rework, cleaning and functional test separate cost drivers (pp. 219–248/PDF 258–287). Its historical prices and rates must be re-estimated for a current line. Choosing a technology solely from bare-board area misses the placement/solder/turn/rework route (pp. 240–243/PDF 279–282).
5. **Outlier operations matter.** The source's connector-board example attributes $1.97 of its $5.10 modeled assembly cost to one connector that had to be hand inserted and soldered on the underside (p. 252/PDF 291). The power-supply case (pp. 253–255/PDF 292–294) attributes much of a $29.08 *modeled manual assembly cost* to eight jumpers, 29 radial insertions, a large capacitor mount, two power transistors, and 17 odd components; Figure 6.16/PDF 293 was visually checked. These are examples of *operation concentration*, not transfer-ready Temper savings.
6. **Mixing PTH and SMT adds routes and handling.** The book walks single-side, two-side and mixed board sequences, including stencil, adhesive, placement, curing, reflow, flip, PTH insertion, wave or manual solder, cleaning and test. It also notes specialized through-hole/reflow variants (pp. 237–243/PDF 276–282). A proposed change should compare *actual available process routes*, including fixture, mask, thermal profile, selective solder, inspection and rework, rather than assume every SMT/PTH mix needs the exact historic sequence shown.
7. **Test and rework are process costs, not afterthoughts.** Bare-board electrical inspection/test, PCBA in-circuit access, and functional interface tests are different (pp. 223, 226–227, 239–240/PDF 262, 265–266, 278–279). Accessible test points, fixture requirements and replacement access should be designed early. An expensive assembly that is scrapped because rework is inaccessible can lose any part-count saving (p. 240/PDF 279).
8. **Mold tool complexity must be compared with cycle time and assembly.** Uniform main walls, drafted draw direction, core/side-pull/lifter/unscrewing needs, parting, ejection, gates and number of cavities all determine economics. Cooling time in the simplified model scales approximately with wall thickness squared; a thicker boss can drive the entire cycle and sink/warp (pp. 343–348, 363–366/PDF 382–387, 402–405). The heater-core cover example deliberately *adds* rib features and mold cost to remove thick pads and reduce the modeled processing cost (pp. 363–364/PDF 402–403). Its historical monetary example should not be used as a current quote.
9. **Snap/press fit savings carry joint-performance obligations.** Integral catches may replace separate fasteners, but a molded press fit can relax; snap integrity depends on polymer, geometry, orientation and cycling. Ultrasonic joining offers a production route with joint geometry/fixture cost; it changes serviceability (pp. 366–368/PDF 405–407). The book cautions against a rule that minimizes only the molded part while making the assembly worse (p. 366/PDF 405).
10. **Sheet-metal shape is a process sequence.** Dedicated dies favor high volumes and tool investment; turret punching/profile cutting plus press-brake bending can favor lower quantities but require more operations. A formed part needs a feasible flat blank, nest, pierce, louver/rib operation, bend sequence and tool access. A feature that is too close to a bend can require post-bend piercing or machining; complex reverse bends may lock a forming die (pp. 375, 390–392, 407–418/PDF 414, 429–431, 446–457). The book's bend-radius and edge-spacing numbers were derived from example ductilities/processes: validate with actual material temper, thickness, tooling and supplier, especially for cosmetic or protective-earth surfaces.

## Interpretation limits that matter for Temper

- The theoretical minimum part count intentionally abstracts from design and service requirements (p. 82/PDF 121). That abstraction helps generate alternatives, but it **cannot override** insulating barriers, PE continuity, thermal-expansion allowances, cleaning/service access, replaceable safety parts, or fault containment. A part that passes all three deletion prompts may still be required after full product verification.
- The PCB chapter's components and insertion economics reflect its publication era; e.g., DIPs/axial machinery and historic labor, solder and fault-rate tables. Current process capabilities, packaging, selective solder, lead-free profiles, regulatory requirements and quotes need fresh vendor-specific evidence. The book gives *what to count*, not current numerical thresholds.
- Its injection and sheet-metal examples provide useful physics and route logic; universal wall ratios, bend spacings, part costs or cycle times cannot be copied to a specific resin, filler, sheet alloy, finish or tool without supplier/material validation.
- There is an internal arithmetic inconsistency in the heater-core-cover worked example: it states a 2 mm main wall (p. 363/PDF 402), then gives a proposed rib thickness as two-thirds of the main wall **or 1.67 mm** (p. 364/PDF 403); two-thirds of 2 mm is 1.33 mm. Treat the qualitative mechanism as useful and independently calculate any geometry.
- A lower operation count does not prove lower actual yield loss or better reliability. The text offers case correlations and modeled fault costs; Temper needs measured pilot yield, inspection coverage, thermal/EMI/electrical behavior, and service trials before such claims.
