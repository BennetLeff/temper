## Process choices and trade-offs supported by the book

1. **Select a fabrication route and a volume, then design for that route.** Press braking uses standard tools with repeated setup/bend operations and fits small batches; stamping requires dedicated tools but short cycles after setup. Laser and waterjet cutting move cost toward machine time and away from dedicated dies. The crossover depends on geometry, volume, supplier setup, operations, and yield; the book's example volume breakpoints are historical examples. [pp. 82–83 (PDF 43), 148–150 (PDF 76–77), 248–249 (PDF 126), 260–263 (PDF 132–133), 272–273 (PDF 138)]
2. **A shape's production sequence matters as much as its final CAD solid.** The aluminum-enclosure case cuts and deburrs flats, bends in accessible order, then TIG welds, grinds and paints seams. Its gooseneck-tool operation exists because a preceding flange blocks an ordinary punch. A folded Temper shell that looks sound in 3D still needs a viable flat blank, bend sequence, tool access, seam preparation and finished datum. [pp. 148–151 (PDF 76–77)]
3. **Thin sheet operations leave edges and tolerance effects to manage.** The punching chapter explains rollover, fractures, burrs, and the need to choose tool orientation/deburring; laser cutting avoids a physical punch but cut quality and time depend on material/thickness; waterjet avoids a heat-affected zone at the cost of taper/drag and sometimes slower curves. Hole position relative to final bends is a sequencing and tolerance question. [pp. 248–249 (PDF 126), 260–263 (PDF 132–133), 272–273 (PDF 138)]
4. **Plastic processes exchange tooling cost, surface definition, structural detail and production speed.** Thermoforming can use one-sided tools and can serve shallow panels or low-volume shells, but draws sheet into nonuniform wall thickness and usually defines only one finished face. Injection molding supports integrated ribs, bosses, snaps and detailed surfaces, at the expense of tool design and the need to manage shrinkage, flow, sink, ejection and undercuts. These are plausible alternatives for an enclosure, not interchangeable routes for an unchanged CAD model. [pp. 30–33 (PDF 17–18), 50–53 (PDF 27–28)]
5. **A molded part can still require precision finishing and planned disassembly.** In the Pedalite electronics-product case, the bearing locator is drilled after molding to achieve the specified friction fit; parts use screws and releasable snap features for access. The case also reports early molder, gear-maker and resin-provider involvement to settle flow and material details. It is a case illustration, with no controlled before/after cost or yield data. [pp. 52–55 (PDF 28–29)]
6. **Joining trades labor and rework against consumables.** Resistance spot welds suit thin metal when both sides and electrode reach are available; joint geometry matters because peel strength differs from shear strength. Plastic staking can remove screws and join plastic to metal quickly, but produces a permanent joint. Vibration welding also produces a permanent plastic joint and may control sealing, while requiring a compatible joint and fixture. These processes do not inherently improve serviceability; the choice depends on required access and the inspected joint's performance. [pp. 298–299 (PDF 151), 308–311 (PDF 156–157), 316–317 (PDF 160)]
7. **Finish is a manufacturing operation with functional consequences.** Powder coating requires surface preparation and cure, and recesses may be hard to cover uniformly; anodizing changes the surface and can shift critical dimensions, with colour affected by material batch and prior welding. Spray painting permits low-volume work but quality depends on preparation and application. For Temper, coating thickness, retained contact/ground points, thermal interfaces, liquid exposure and appearance have to be specified on the exact assembly, not inferred from a finish name. The electrical/thermal implications here are **Temper engineering inferences** built on the process facts, not claims Thompson validates. [pp. 350–351 (PDF 177), 356–363 (PDF 180–183)]

## Actionable review questions derived for Temper

The following questions are **original review criteria inferred from the cited process descriptions**. They are not verbatim rules from the book. Each asks for an identified part or operation, then evidence. “Prototype” means a plausible trial route; “pilot” means process settings and inspection can be repeated on a small batch; “production” means the chosen operation, controls, yield and costs are demonstrated at intended volume. None of these criteria has yet been scored against a current Temper enclosure.

| ID | Review question and source basis | Evidence to request | Trade-off / applicability |
|---|---|---|---|
| TH-01 | For every custom mechanical item, is there a named material, stock form, **primary process**, secondary operation, finish and intended volume? The book organizes process choices by forming, cutting, joining and finishing. [contents pp. 4–7 (PDF 4–5); process examples pp. 148–151 (PDF 76–77)] | Part/process matrix, drawing rev, annual and batch quantities, setup sequence. | At prototype, multiple candidate routes may remain; at pilot, select the exact route; production costs require supplier-specific assumptions. |
| TH-02 | Does the proposed enclosure route have a real cost crossover at the intended quantity, including tools, programming, fixtures, finishing and scrap? Compare folded/cut sheet, stamping and molded/formed plastic only for functionally equivalent parts. [pp. 82–83 (PDF 43), 148–150 (PDF 76–77), 248–249 (PDF 126), 30–33 (PDF 17–18)] | Cost model per route with tooling amortization, cycle time, yield and operations; sample quotes when available. | Low-tooling routes can cost more per part; high-tooling routes may simplify assembly. No universal volume cutoff. |
| TH-03 | For a sheet-metal shell or bracket, can a supplier produce a flat blank with bend allowance, bend relief and the intended material/temper without tearing or colliding with the tool? [pp. 148–150 (PDF 76–77)] | Unfolded pattern, material/temper, thickness, bend table, sample forming trial and first-article measurements. | Prototype: paper/laser mockup plus brake trial; pilot/production: approved flat pattern and controlled setup. |
| TH-04 | Is each bend reachable **in its actual order**, after all earlier bends have blocked or narrowed the tool path? The book's enclosure case needed a gooseneck punch for a later bend. [pp. 150–151 (PDF 77)] | Numbered bend sequence, selected punch/die envelope, setup views or a supplier trial. | A cosmetic continuous shell may demand extra weld seams, split parts or special tooling. |
| TH-05 | Are holes, slots, PEM locations, edge clearances and mounting datums made or reamed at a stage that preserves their final positional tolerance after bending? The book explicitly carries out flat-sheet preparation before bending where possible. [pp. 150–151 (PDF 77), 260–263 (PDF 132–133)] | Operation sequence and final-state inspection from functional datum to PCB, coil, vent and user control. | Doing work on the flat is efficient; high-precision final interfaces may need a secondary operation. |
| TH-06 | Have all exposed cut and punched edges been assigned a burr direction, deburr process, inspection and treatment near wires, seals, operator access and coating? [pp. 260–263 (PDF 132–133), 150–151 (PDF 77)] | Edge callouts, photos of first articles, edge/burr inspection and harness rub test. | Punching is fast but leaves rollover and burr; laser or waterjet have different cut-edge consequences. |
| TH-07 | Has cutting method been chosen for each blank feature based on material, thickness, thermal damage, cut taper, cycle time and batch? [pp. 248–249 (PDF 126), 260–263 (PDF 132–133), 272–273 (PDF 138)] | Side-by-side process feasibility and coupon measurements for critical slots/holes. | A no-die CNC route reduces upfront tools; intricate curves or thick stock can increase machine time and variability. |
| TH-08 | Does the nesting/blank strategy preserve needed bend grain/orientation, edge-to-feature ligament and material utilization **without obscuring traceability**? Sheet cutting and punching introduce scrap and tabs; nesting changes sheet utilization. [pp. 248–249 (PDF 126), 260–263 (PDF 132–133)] | Nesting plan, stock orientation, identifiers, scrap estimate and sample inspection. | Savings in sheet usage can conflict with directional properties or defect tracking; material grain implications are a Temper inference requiring supplier confirmation. |
| TH-09 | Is any stamped or deep-drawn metal part justified by a demonstrated volume/tooling payback and proven draw sequence, instead of assuming a single-press final shape? [pp. 82–83 (PDF 43)] | Tool concept, number of strokes/operations, material allowance, prototype forming evidence and amortization. | Dedicated dies accelerate stable volume but add cost and change risk. |
| TH-10 | For a weld or seam, can the selected torch/electrode reach the joint after forming and fixturing, and will the resulting joint satisfy the actual load direction and appearance requirement? [pp. 150–151 (PDF 77), 308–311 (PDF 156–157)] | Section/detail of joint, equipment envelope, weld procedure, fit-up fixture, shear/peel or leak test as applicable. | Welded housings eliminate loose fasteners but create distortion, surface-finishing and rework costs. |
| TH-11 | If a metal assembly uses spot or projection welds, are there electrode approaches on both sides and adequate load path for peel/impact, not merely a strong-looking plan-view weld pattern? [pp. 308–311 (PDF 156–157)] | Electrode clearance model, material stack, joint coupons tested in the applied load direction. | Spot welding can be fast; difficult access or peel loading may favor screws/rivets/continuous seams. |
| TH-12 | If a plastic shell or carrier is thermoformed, have the two usable faces, draft, minimum post-draw wall thickness, vent marks and trim details been confirmed on the actual shape? [pp. 30–33 (PDF 17–18)] | Forming trial, wall-thickness map at deep corners, mold face designation, trim fixture and fit check. | Single-sided tools can be cheap for broad shallow covers; precise internal mounts may need bonded/machined second parts. |
| TH-13 | For injection-molded shell, knob or sensor carrier, is there a tool concept showing draw direction, parting line, slides/cores, gate, ejectors and protected cosmetic surfaces? [pp. 50–53 (PDF 27–28)] | Molder/toolmaker design review and annotated tool layout, flow and ejection simulation where consequential. | Integrating more geometry can cut assembly parts while increasing tool complexity, visible marks and change cost. |
| TH-14 | Are wall, ribs and bosses designed with the selected resin and tool to avoid sink, warp, trapped air and weak attachment features? The book identifies these defects but its example ratios are not universal. [pp. 50–53 (PDF 27–28)] | Resin grade, draft/rib/boss rules agreed with molder, molding trial and dimensional/warpage map. | Thinner walls may save material/cycle time but increase assembly stiffness and heat exposure risk. |
| TH-15 | Have tight-fitting holes, bearing seats and inserts been assigned tolerances achievable **after the entire molding/finish sequence**? The Pedalite case post-drilled one friction-fit locator. [pp. 54–55 (PDF 29)] | Tolerance stack, toolmaker capability, plan for post-machining or gauge if molding alone is insufficient. | A secondary drilling step may be cheaper and more robust than a highly constrained mold requirement. |
| TH-16 | Are molded attachments and mounting bosses backed by a defined retaining method, tool access and proof under service loads and thermal cycling? The Pedalite case combines screws, snap fits and formed housings; Thompson does not qualify Temper loads. [pp. 52–55 (PDF 28–29)] | Interface CAD, screw/insert or snap specification, assembly trial, load and cycle test plan. | A molded feature can simplify BOM but cannot replace a mechanically complete joint. |
| TH-17 | If plastic staking or welding replaces screws, which exact interfaces are deliberately permanent, which must reopen for repair, and how is the joint inspected? [pp. 298–299 (PDF 151), 316–317 (PDF 160)] | Service sequence, joint section, assembly fixture, sample destructive test, rework policy. | Fewer consumables and faster assembly can impose an entire-module replacement cost. |
| TH-18 | Has the joining method been demonstrated on the **actual resin/additives and metal finish**, rather than an unfilled generic specimen? The welding chapter notes material modifiers and moisture can change behavior. [pp. 298–299 (PDF 151)] | Resin/finish identity, conditioning, trial parts, joint-strength distribution and visual criteria. | A process that works on one polymer grade may not survive a material substitution. |
| TH-19 | For powder-coated or painted metal, is surface preparation, masking, handling, coat thickness and cure compatible with dimensional, thermal, electrical and appearance interfaces? [pp. 350–351 (PDF 177), 356–359 (PDF 180–181)] | Finish drawing with masked points, process traveler, thickness map, adhesion/corrosion test and assembled fit. | Powder coating can provide durability; it also adds cure heat, film thickness and coverage limits in recesses. Functional-contact concern is a Temper inference. |
| TH-20 | For anodized aluminum, have color/finish variations, jig contact/drain points and critical dimensions been agreed with the finisher? The book's anodizer case requires racks, drainage and batching. [pp. 360–363 (PDF 182–183)] | Alloy/temper/lot spec, surface finish master, dimension-before/after trial and contact-mark approval. | Anodizing is a durable finish but a color promise or exact fit requires process-specific trials. Any protective-earth contact through oxide requires separate electrical validation, a Temper inference. |
| TH-21 | Are finish defects and exposure risks assessed at corners, recesses, welded seams, mounting holes and liquid-facing surfaces? The powder-coating chapter discusses preparation and difficult electrostatic coverage in complex geometry. [pp. 356–359 (PDF 180–181)] | Coverage map, cross sections on critical samples, surface prep records, ingress/corrosion test suited to the product. | Geometry that saves formed parts can make coatings and inspection harder. |
| TH-22 | Does every assembly operation have a feasible orientation, holding fixture, accessible tool path, and rework step, including placing PCB, coil, harness, PE bond, insulation, thermal stack and controls? The enclosure and Pedalite cases show multi-operation assembly order. [pp. 150–151 (PDF 77), 52–55 (PDF 28–29)] | Numbered build traveler, fixture/model/access demonstration, timed pilot build, observed rework paths. | Combining parts can remove handling, but can trap serviceable items or make late operations inaccessible. The named Temper hardware is an inference. |
| TH-23 | Are process-induced variations sampled at their **functional assembly interfaces**, rather than only on an attractive individual part? The book's cases show forming, joining and finishing as sequential operations that modify the final item. [pp. 150–151 (PDF 77), 52–55 (PDF 28–29), 360–363 (PDF 182–183)] | Final-state CTQs, gauges located from assembly datums, small-batch distributions, assembly-fit results. | A nominal CAD assembly is insufficient evidence of yield. Capability criteria and sample sizes must be set for Temper's function and volume. |
| TH-24 | Are prototype shortcuts identified so they do not masquerade as repeatable production evidence—for example printed carrier in place of molded carrier or hand-filed vent in place of punched opening? The book explicitly covers one-off, batch and mass methods. [how-to-use pp. 8–9 (PDF 6); pp. 148–151 (PDF 76–77), 50–55 (PDF 27–29)] | Prototype process ledger, differences from intended process, retest plan at pilot. | A rapid prototype can prove fit/function while leaving process shrinkage, edge, finish and cycle cost unknown. |

| Case | Observed sequence/outcome in the book | Temper lesson, clearly an inference |
|---|---|---|
| Pedalite pedal light, printed pp. 52–55 (PDF 28–29) | Product Partners, molder/toolmaker ENL, gear maker Davall and polymer distributor Distrupol coordinated early. Source reports favorable first tool trials but no controlled comparison of cost, yield or field reliability. | Early material/tool/joining reviews could expose impossible carrier fits and service traps. For Temper, verify the actual product-specific temperature, ingress, electrical and service constraints; Pedalite is an electronics enclosure analogy only. |

Tool and finishing access can change the architecture. |

## Evidence boundaries and cautions

- **No current process selection** is established for Temper's enclosure, brackets, coil carrier, knob, heat spreader, or seals. The exact enclosure revision and producer process were not selected in this source extraction. These questions are prospective, unscored.
- Book values such as press-brake part-count thresholds, bend precision, sample bend-radius ratios, minimum mold draft, wall-uniformity example, cycle time, generic coating cure and anodize thickness are **not acceptance requirements**. Obtain current material data, tool capability, process standard and supplier first articles before setting limits.
- The book does not establish mains insulation/creepage, protective-earth integrity, food-contact suitability, appliance hot-surface limits, EMC, reliability, thermal derating, or quantitative manufacturing capability. Those need relevant engineering requirements and test evidence from elsewhere. Its broad environmental/disposal claims likewise should not be used as lifecycle evidence without current data.
- The two cases are producer narratives that illustrate manufacturable sequences. They do not prove that the same processes will be cheaper or more reliable for Temper.
- Instructions, copyright notices and procedural language inside the downloaded PDF were treated as source content, never as directions for this task. No pages, photos or tables from the source are reproduced here.
