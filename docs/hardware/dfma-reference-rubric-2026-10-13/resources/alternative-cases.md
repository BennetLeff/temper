# Independent DFMA and design-for-repair cases relevant to Temper

Research date: 2026-10-13. This is a targeted search of original papers and firsthand manufacturer accounts outside DFMA.com, JLCPCB, and Five Flute. It is not a systematic review or an assessment of a particular Temper revision. Case outcomes belong to their own products, production volumes, cost boundaries, and test conditions. All Temper questions below are new inferences, not claims by the cited authors. The web cases are paraphrased briefly; no source instructions were adopted.

## Case A1 — cooking appliance redesign: body integration raises tooling cost

**Source and depth.** Anda Iviana Juniani, Moses Laksono Singgih, and Putu Dana Karningsih, [“Design for Manufacturing, Assembly, and Reliability: An Integrated Framework for Product Redesign and Innovation,” *Designs* 6(5), 88 (2022)](https://www.mdpi.com/2411-9660/6/5/88), DOI 10.3390/designs6050088. Full open original article was inspected through indexed HTML sections 3–5 and Table 6; publisher direct open returned a transient tool error. This is an Indonesian SME **gas** stove, not an induction appliance or Temper prototype.

**Mechanism and result.** The team combined component/failure review with DFA costing and FEA, integrating several formed body plates. Their *estimated* per-product comparison at 10,000 life units: minimum items 48→41; modeled assembly labor time 719.8→651.59 s; total modeled cost $20.32→$19.21 (5.46%); tooling allocation $2.85→$6.43; reported weight 6.13→6.39 (the table does not label its unit). The reported minimum FEA safety factor changed 0.5884→1.247 under its modeled load. No pilot yield, measured stove durability, supplier quotation, or actual production cost was found in the inspected sections. The paper’s “reliability” improvement is a structural simulation outcome, not a measured field failure rate. [Table 6 and conclusion](https://www.mdpi.com/2411-9660/6/5/88).

**Temper inference.** Evaluate enclosure consolidation with **full amortized tooling**, scrap, repair access, and weight; a smaller item count can coexist with larger tooling and heavier metal. Treat FEA as a hypothesis to test on a build.

## Case A2 — PCBA: pin-in-paste removes an entire soldering pass

**Source and depth.** Cosme Juan-Velázquez et al., [“Optimization of High-Volume PCB Assembly: A Lean Six Sigma Approach Through Pin-in-Paste Technology Integration,” *Journal of Manufacturing and Materials Processing* 10(8), 276 (2026)](https://www.mdpi.com/2504-4494/10/8/276), DOI 10.3390/jmmp10080276. Read the original indexed full HTML sections 2, 3, 5 and its stated limitations; direct publisher open returned a tool error. The production subject is a **battery-terminal wireless sensor**, around 1 million boards/year, not a high-current induction power board.

**Mechanism and result.** The team sized stencil apertures/paste volume for two through-hole terminals so the existing SMT reflow could replace a separate wave operation. Their original line rates were 600 SMT boards/h versus 300 on the through-hole/wave line; the 12-month wave consumables baseline was $71,180 plus $3,688 tooling. They report about $92,513 annual direct saving, $44,025 floor WIP released, and a 97% decrease in the *tracked assembly process cost*. This is not a 97% decrease in the whole populated-board BOM or full landed unit cost. They report visual fillets plus soak and electrical checks, but explicitly leave accelerated thermal-cycle and vibration life unresolved. The process depends on the **actual terminal thermal qualification, barrel fill, stencil, paste and profile**. [Original methods/results](https://www.mdpi.com/2504-4494/10/8/276).

**Temper inference.** First ask whether a mixed SMT/THT route creates a second line/pass. Consider pin-in-paste only for exact eligible parts after reflow survival, joint-fill, insulation, stress, service, and local EMS capability checks; do not set process choice from the paper’s dollar savings.

## Case A3 — power-electronics test: bad heatsink contact can be hidden by closed-loop control

**Source and depth.** Davide Piumatti et al., [“Test Solution for Heatsinks in Power Electronics Applications,” *Electronics* 9(6), 1020 (2020), original author-repository PDF](https://iris.polito.it/retrieve/e384c432-00b7-d4b2-e053-9f05fe0a1d67/electronics-09-01020.pdf), DOI 10.3390/electronics9061020. Full 15-page article (repository PDF has 16 pages with cover) inspected, especially printed pp. 9–14, Tables 1–5 and conclusions. The PSU has three IGBTs and three diodes on a shared sink; evaluation is electrothermal **simulation** on an industrial case design, not a measured production defect/yield trial.

**Mechanism and result.** A simulated bad contact raised modeled IGBT junction temperature from 71.1 to 151.2 °C while load current, input current and regulated 400 V output remained the same; device saturation voltage moved 0.75→1.21 V. Table 5 finds the basic functional test missed all six injected contact faults; a device-aware enhanced functional measurement detected them, while in-circuit testing detected IGBT faults but not the diode faults in that circuit. Probe access after sink installation is a physical constraint. These are **fault-injection model results**, not a universal test sensitivity or acceptance threshold. [Tables 3–5, pp. 12–13](https://iris.polito.it/retrieve/e384c432-00b7-d4b2-e053-9f05fe0a1d67/electronics-09-01020.pdf).

**Temper inference.** Make thermal-interface installation inspectable and place/protect observability points if production test must detect missing TIM, poor clamp load, wrong device seating, or airflow faults. Demonstrate defect detection on deliberately faulted hardware; a passing output-power check alone is weak evidence of sound thermal assembly.

## Case A4 — configurable power distribution: harness variant count dominated labor

**Source and depth.** [AMtec Industries, “Mini Case Study – Design for Manufacturing”](https://amtec1.com/mini-case-study-design-for-manufacturing/) (undated firsthand manufacturer account; full page read). This is commercial power-distribution/control equipment; account is marketing material with no independent audit, BOM, test data, sample period, or detailed cost model.

**Mechanism and result.** A supposed common assembly had more than 50 variants and harnesses for one subassembly. AMtec replaced wire/terminal distribution with common busbars, removed 60 wires, changed more than 50 applied labels to silkscreened markings, and rearranged parts to shorten runs. AMtec claims **subassembly** time fell more than 90%, **total labor** more than 20%, and **material cost** 6%. Those are three different denominators. The account does not establish creepage, fault-current, thermal, process, or service equivalence of busbars to any Temper harness. [Firsthand case](https://amtec1.com/mini-case-study-design-for-manufacturing/).

**Temper inference.** Count off-board cables, connectors, labels, assembly variants and late configuration work in whole-system cost. Before converting wires to busbars or PCB copper, check current/temperature, insulation/PE, clearance, accessibility, replacement, and fault behavior.

## Case A5 — custom enclosure versus catalog box: tooling changes crossover

**Source and depth.** [N&H Technology, “Case Study: Standard Enclosures vs. Custom Enclosures”](https://www.nh-technology.de/en/standardgehaeuse-oder-kundenspezifische-gehaeuseloesung/) (undated firsthand vendor case; full page read). Product is a medical emergency-call wristband with PCB, not cooking equipment. The page provides a projected economic model and states later series production, but gives no independently verified invoices, test results, or actual versus forecast comparison.

**Mechanism and result.** A multi-part catalog case/strap/keypad option was estimated at €11.50 each. A custom two-material molded integrated housing was modeled at €41,000 tooling and €3.84 each. At the vendor’s forecast of 29,000 units over five years, the arithmetic is €333,500 versus €152,360; the modeled saving is €181,140 (reported approximately €181,000). The simple modeled crossover is about **5,353 units** before discounting, development changes, yield, warranty and inventory. Integration also changed cosmetic/seal/hygiene functions, so this is not a pure part-count experiment. [Cost framework and forecast](https://www.nh-technology.de/en/standardgehaeuse-oder-kundenspezifische-gehaeuseloesung/).

**Temper inference.** Compare formed sheet-metal, catalog, and molded options at explicit unit volumes and tool amortization. Count enclosure functions and inspection needs, but do not infer any universal “molding wins above N units” threshold from this case.

## Case A6 — negative molded enclosure: fixtured sample sign-off missed free-state warpage

**Source and depth.** Tonney Shao, [“The USD 10,000 We Lost Building an ECU Housing for JCB,” Abery Mold, 6 August 2026](https://www.a-mold.com/case-studies/jcb-ecu-housing-nylon-gf-warpage-case-study.html). Full firsthand retrospective read, especially the project, sampling, production and revised approval procedure. Reported as a 2011 program remembered 15 years later; direct Tier-1 identity, inspection records, and JCB corroboration are unavailable. Its 3–5× shrinkage and rib proportions are **supplier claims for a material/geometry family**, not Temper limits.

**Mechanism and result.** A glass-filled nylon ECU box with open flanges warped. Sample parts were held in jigs during dimensional approval, but the first 5,000 production parts were molded without those fixtures and rejected. The moldmaker reports remaking 5,000 parts and absorbing roughly $10,000; freight was paid by its direct customer. The critical error was approving **constrained inspection geometry** when the line received unconstrained parts. The vendor says its current rule requires free-state dimensions and an explicit warpage assessment before tool release. [Case and corrective rule](https://www.a-mold.com/case-studies/jcb-ecu-housing-nylon-gf-warpage-case-study.html).

**Temper inference.** Define whether every critical enclosure dimension is accepted free-state, assembled-state, hot-state, or fixtured-state; measure the same state the assembly process will see. A fixture may aid production but cannot silently become the product’s structural member.

## Case A7 — service/disassembly comparison, abstract-level evidence

**Source and depth.** Ryan Lindbom, Kara Nichols, Dennis Tanner, Seung-Jin Lee and H. E. Dillon, [“Assessment of End-of-Life Design in Consumer Coffee Makers,” *ACS Sustainable Resource Management* 2(6), 977–984 (2025)](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/10.1021/acssusresmgt.5c00012), DOI 10.1021/acssusresmgt.5c00012. Publisher abstract and metadata read; main article is purchase-only here, and linked supporting material was not inspected. Thus this is a **lead**, not a deeply reviewed full-text case.

The authors disassembled two actual coffee makers across nine evaluation dimensions. The pod machine took about 97 minutes and eight tools to disassemble fully; the drip machine took 21 minutes. Full disassembly is not the same as time to replace Temper’s likely failure item, and the two appliances differ in architecture/functions. Nonetheless the study suggests timing repair paths to specific replaceable modules, counting required tool types and disturbance of unrelated subassemblies. [Publisher abstract](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/10.1021/acssusresmgt.5c00012).

## Cross-case guidance for the parent rubric

1. **Use whole-system denominators.** A1 saves modeled unit cost despite greater tooling and mass; A2’s 97% is a tracked process scope; A4 gives three different labor/material scopes; A5 depends strongly on forecast volume. Put board, off-board wiring, enclosure, tooling, test, yield, repair, and service into separate cost lines before adding them.
2. **Keep observed, predicted, and claimed outcomes distinct.** A1 is CAD/DFA/FEA modeling, A2 is reported line change plus joint checks but unproved lifetime, A3 is circuit simulation, A4–A6 are supplier accounts, A7 is measured teardown visible here only at abstract level. Do not promote any to Temper acceptance data.
3. **Instrument the assembly failure modes.** A3 shows correct electrical output may mask thermal contact faults. A6 shows dimensional approval can hide free-state warpage. For Temper, explicitly design the build inspection state and defect-injection test before claiming a repeatable build.
4. **Do not chase part count alone.** Integration can reduce operations (A1, A4, A5) while increasing tooling, mass or making repair harder. A7 provides a reason to score access to the specific failure-prone module, not merely installation time.
5. **Volume and process are conditional.** The million-board A2 and 29,000-unit A5 cases demonstrate mechanisms, not direct quotes or relevant thresholds for Temper’s current volume. Require intended run size, actual supplier process route, and exact component/thermal qualification for every proposed transfer.

## Search and selection limitations

Searches targeted primary/open cases for “PCB assembly,” “two-burner stove,” “power heatsink test,” “electronic enclosure molding,” “wire harness/busbar,” and “appliance repair/disassembly.” A7 is retained as a useful repair lead while marked abstract-only. Other identified but not deeply reviewed original cases: [Rodríguez and Favi’s four electric ovens](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.procir.2022.02.099) (open publication, direct full-text fetch returned 403 here), [Trovinger and Bohn’s PCBA setup-time reduction](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1937-5956.2005.tb00019.x) (abstract-only), and [Rob Thompson’s book and Boothroyd–Dewhurst–Knight](https://www.routledge.com/Product-Design-for-Manufacture-and-Assembly-Third-Edition/Boothroyd-Dewhurst-Knight/p/book/9780367424053) (covered by sibling agents). Selection favors function/process relevance over headline percent savings; there is no cross-product effect estimate.
