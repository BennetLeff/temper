# Product taste for Temper

Use taste as a judgment about the whole cooking experience: what a person can understand, do, clean, trust, and maintain. Ground a recommendation in observed behavior and product constraints. Do not infer the owner's aesthetic preferences from MIT coursework.

1. Start with the decision at hand and the intended cook, use context, and current design evidence. Translate needs into observable behavior. For example, describe what a cook should recognize or accomplish when placing a pan, changing heat, encountering a fault, cleaning a spill, or resuming after interruption. Mark untested assumptions.
2. Compare plausible alternatives on the priorities that matter for that decision: cooking control and feedback, heat and airflow, pan stability, spill paths and cleaning, access for assembly and repair, durability, appearance, and cost. Record why an option wins and what it gives up; scale the comparison to the stakes. Do not invent a required alternative count or silently promote visual preference over function.
3. For consequential choices, use a small FREEDPARRC record as a thinking aid: functional requirement; ergonomics and environment; design parameter; analysis; reference; risk; countermeasure. Start at the product or module level and add detail only where a decision needs it. A risk and countermeasure remain open until a relevant test or analysis closes them.
4. Choose the cheapest prototype that can resolve the live uncertainty: full-size interface mockup for reach and legibility, spill/cleaning sample for seams, physical stack for pan/coil/sensor fit, instrumented module for heat and airflow. State the predicted result and observable success criterion before testing. Watch representative users when the question concerns interpretation or behavior; record what they did separately from what they said and from the designer's inference.
5. Return a decision record proportionate to the request: requirement, options, evidence, tradeoff, unresolved risk, next discriminating test, and owner of the decision. Flag contradictions among enclosure, PCB, firmware, thermal, and service assumptions. Preserve a user's explicit priorities when they are known.

The MIT material supplies design process, not an appliance usability standard or a Temper verdict. For source links, precise slide/page pointers, and the distinction between course teaching and Temper applications, read [evidence notes](references/evidence.md) when citing or adapting the method. Get electrical safety, insulation, temperature, and compliance criteria from applicable standards, component and manufacturer data, and qualified review.

For a flush or concealed feature, compare its visual benefit with the actual tolerance, soil/liquid path, cleaning access, and repair sequence. Hidden fasteners and permanent bonding are different decisions. Preserve the desired appearance while making their consequences explicit.
